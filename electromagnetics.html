Electromagnetics extends electrostatics to time-dependent electric and magnetic fields. Here the changing fields couple through Faraday's law and Ampère–Maxwell's law, permitting electromagnetic-wave propagation. The starting equations are the same Maxwell equations explored on the main page.
01 / TIME-DEPENDENT EQUATIONS

Coupled electric and magnetic fields

Macroscopic Maxwell equations

\[\begin{aligned} \nabla\cdot\mathbf D&=\rho_f,\\ \nabla\cdot\mathbf B&=0,\\ \nabla\times\mathbf E&=-\frac{\partial\mathbf B}{\partial t},\\ \nabla\times\mathbf H&=\mathbf J_f+\frac{\partial\mathbf D}{\partial t}. \end{aligned}\]

Concept: time-dependent \(\mathbf B\) generates electric circulation; current and time-dependent \(\mathbf D\) generate magnetic circulation. The divergence equations constrain the sources and net flux.

Derive free-charge continuity from Maxwell's equations

Take the divergence of the Ampère–Maxwell equation:

\[\nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf H) =\nabla\cdot\mathbf J_f+\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(\nabla\cdot\mathbf D).\]

Since the divergence of a curl is zero and \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf D=\rho_f\),

\[\boxed{\frac{\partial\rho_f}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot\mathbf J_f=0}.\]

Meaning: free charge is locally conserved. A decrease in charge inside a small volume corresponds to free current leaving it.

02 / WAVE EQUATIONS

Derive the electromagnetic wave equation

Assume a source-free region, \(\rho_f=0\), \(\mathbf J_f=0\), and a homogeneous, isotropic, linear, nondispersive medium with time-independent scalar \(\varepsilon,\mu\). Hence \(\mathbf D=\varepsilon\mathbf E\) and \(\mathbf B=\mu\mathbf H\).

Electric-field wave equation · every major step

Start from Faraday's law and take a curl:

\[\nabla\times\mathbf E=-\mu\frac{\partial\mathbf H}{\partial t},\] \[\nabla\times(\nabla\times\mathbf E) =-\mu\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(\nabla\times\mathbf H).\]

Use source-free Ampère–Maxwell: \(\nabla\times\mathbf H=\varepsilon\,\partial_t\mathbf E\):

\[\nabla\times(\nabla\times\mathbf E)=-\mu\varepsilon\frac{\partial^2\mathbf E}{\partial t^2}.\]

Now apply the vector identity:

\[\nabla\times(\nabla\times\mathbf E)=\nabla(\nabla\cdot\mathbf E)-\nabla^2\mathbf E.\]

Since \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf D=0\) and \(\varepsilon\) is constant, \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf E=0\), so

\[-\nabla^2\mathbf E=-\mu\varepsilon\frac{\partial^2\mathbf E}{\partial t^2}.\] \[\boxed{\nabla^2\mathbf E=\mu\varepsilon\frac{\partial^2\mathbf E}{\partial t^2}}.\]

This is a wave equation with propagation speed

\[\boxed{v=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu\varepsilon}}}.\]

In vacuum, \(v=c=1/\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0}\).

Magnetic-field wave equation

Start from \(\nabla\times\mathbf H=\varepsilon\,\partial_t\mathbf E\) and curl both sides:

\[\nabla\times(\nabla\times\mathbf H)=\varepsilon\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(\nabla\times\mathbf E).\]

Substitute Faraday's law \(\nabla\times\mathbf E=-\mu\,\partial_t\mathbf H\), and use \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf H=0\) (since \(\mathbf B=\mu\mathbf H\) and constant \(\mu\)):

\[-\nabla^2\mathbf H=-\mu\varepsilon\,\partial_t^2\mathbf H.\] \[\boxed{\nabla^2\mathbf H=\mu\varepsilon\frac{\partial^2\mathbf H}{\partial t^2}}.\]

Because \(\mu\) is constant, \(\mathbf B\) obeys the corresponding wave equation. These simple forms generally fail when material properties vary spatially or are dispersive.

03 / PLANE WAVES

Wave direction versus field direction

Plane wave traveling in \(+\hat{\mathbf z}\)

\[\mathbf E(z,t)=E_0\cos(kz-\omega t)\,\hat{\mathbf x}\] \[\mathbf B(z,t)=\frac{E_0}{v}\cos(kz-\omega t)\,\hat{\mathbf y}\]

The field points along \(x\), while the wave propagates along \(z\). Differentiating \(E_x\) with respect to \(z\) is therefore completely consistent.

Derive B, wave speed and wave impedance

Represent a monochromatic wave using a complex amplitude, taking the real part at the end:

\[\mathbf E=\operatorname{Re}\{\mathbf E_0e^{i(\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r-\omega t)}\}.\]

For this wave, \(\nabla\times\mathbf E\leftrightarrow i\mathbf k\times\mathbf E_0\) and \(\partial_t\mathbf B\leftrightarrow-i\omega\mathbf B_0\). Faraday's law gives

\[i\mathbf k\times\mathbf E_0=i\omega\mathbf B_0,\] \[\boxed{\mathbf B_0=\frac{1}{\omega}\mathbf k\times\mathbf E_0}.\]

For propagation along \(+\hat{\mathbf z}\), \(\mathbf k=k\hat{\mathbf z}\), and polarization \(\mathbf E_0=E_0\hat{\mathbf x}\):

\[\mathbf B_0=\frac{kE_0}{\omega}(\hat{\mathbf z}\times\hat{\mathbf x})=\frac{E_0}{v}\hat{\mathbf y}.\]

Here \(v=\omega/k\). Because \(\mathbf B=\mu\mathbf H\),

\[\boxed{\mathbf H=\frac{1}{\eta}\hat{\mathbf z}\times\mathbf E},\qquad \boxed{\eta=\sqrt{\frac{\mu}{\varepsilon}}}.\]

\(\eta\) is the wave impedance. The fields \(\mathbf E\), \(\mathbf B\), and propagation direction form a right-handed orthogonal triad in this isotropic medium.

04 / ENERGY TRANSPORT

Derive Poynting's theorem

In a linear, isotropic, nondispersive medium with time-independent \(\varepsilon\) and \(\mu\), electromagnetic energy density and flux have particularly simple forms.

From Maxwell's equations to energy conservation

Start with the vector identity

\[\nabla\cdot(\mathbf E\times\mathbf H) =\mathbf H\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf E)-\mathbf E\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf H).\]

Insert Faraday's and Ampère–Maxwell's laws:

\[\nabla\cdot(\mathbf E\times\mathbf H) =-\mathbf H\cdot\frac{\partial\mathbf B}{\partial t} -\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf J_f -\mathbf E\cdot\frac{\partial\mathbf D}{\partial t}.\]

With \(\mathbf B=\mu\mathbf H\), \(\mathbf D=\varepsilon\mathbf E\), and constant \(\varepsilon,\mu\),

\[\mathbf H\cdot\partial_t\mathbf B=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\frac{\mu H^2}{2}\right),\qquad \mathbf E\cdot\partial_t\mathbf D=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\frac{\varepsilon E^2}{2}\right).\]

Move energy-derivative terms to the left:

\[\boxed{\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot\mathbf S=-\mathbf J_f\cdot\mathbf E},\] \[\boxed{u=\frac12\varepsilon E^2+\frac12\mu H^2},\qquad \boxed{\mathbf S=\mathbf E\times\mathbf H}.\]

Meaning: a region loses field energy by transporting it outward (\(\nabla\cdot\mathbf S>0\)) or by doing work on free charges (\(\mathbf J_f\cdot\mathbf E>0\)). For dispersive or lossy matter the simple expression for \(u\) need not apply.

05 / WAVES AT AN INTERFACE

Reflection and transmission at normal incidence

Consider a plane wave traveling from a lossless isotropic medium 1 into medium 2 across the plane \(z=0\), with no free surface charge or current. Their wave impedances are \(\eta_1,\eta_2\). At normal incidence, tangential \(\mathbf E\) and \(\mathbf H\) are continuous.

Derive electric-field reflection and transmission coefficients

Let \(E_i\), \(E_r\), \(E_t\) be complex phasor amplitudes of the incident, reflected and transmitted electric fields, all along \(\hat{\mathbf x}\). Then \(\mathbf H\) for a \(+\hat{\mathbf z}\)-traveling wave is \(+\hat{\mathbf y}E/\eta\), while the reflected wave travels in \(-\hat{\mathbf z}\) and therefore has \(\mathbf H_r=-\hat{\mathbf y}E_r/\eta_1\).

The two interface boundary conditions are

\[E_i+E_r=E_t,\qquad\frac{E_i-E_r}{\eta_1}=\frac{E_t}{\eta_2}.\]

Define \(r=E_r/E_i\) and \(t=E_t/E_i\):

\[1+r=t,\qquad\frac{1-r}{\eta_1}=\frac{1+r}{\eta_2}.\]

Multiply through and solve:

\[\eta_2-\eta_2r=\eta_1+\eta_1r,\] \[\boxed{r=\frac{\eta_2-\eta_1}{\eta_2+\eta_1}},\qquad \boxed{t=\frac{2\eta_2}{\eta_1+\eta_2}}.\]

For real positive impedances and lossless materials, time-averaged power reflection and transmission are

\[\boxed{R=|r|^2},\qquad \boxed{T=\frac{\eta_1}{\eta_2}|t|^2},\qquad R+T=1.\]

Meaning: impedance mismatch generates reflection. The sign of \(r\) indicates whether the reflected electric field reverses its phase. Equal impedances give \(r=0\), even if the wave speeds differ.

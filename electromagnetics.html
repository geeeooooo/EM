Electromagnetics extends electrostatics to time-dependent electric and magnetic fields. Here the changing fields couple through Faraday's law and Ampère–Maxwell's law, permitting electromagnetic-wave propagation. The starting equations are the same Maxwell equations explored on the main page.
01 / TIME-DEPENDENT EQUATIONS

Coupled electric and magnetic fields

Macroscopic Maxwell equations

\[\begin{aligned} \nabla\cdot\mathbf D&=\rho_f,\\ \nabla\cdot\mathbf B&=0,\\ \nabla\times\mathbf E&=-\frac{\partial\mathbf B}{\partial t},\\ \nabla\times\mathbf H&=\mathbf J_f+\frac{\partial\mathbf D}{\partial t}. \end{aligned}\]

Concept: time-dependent \(\mathbf B\) generates electric circulation; current and time-dependent \(\mathbf D\) generate magnetic circulation. The divergence equations constrain the sources and net flux.

Derive free-charge continuity from Maxwell's equations

Take the divergence of the Ampère–Maxwell equation:

\[\nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf H) =\nabla\cdot\mathbf J_f+\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(\nabla\cdot\mathbf D).\]

Since the divergence of a curl is zero and \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf D=\rho_f\),

\[\boxed{\frac{\partial\rho_f}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot\mathbf J_f=0}.\]

Meaning: free charge is locally conserved. A decrease in charge inside a small volume corresponds to free current leaving it.

02 / WAVE EQUATIONS

Derive the electromagnetic wave equation

Assume a source-free region, \(\rho_f=0\), \(\mathbf J_f=0\), and a homogeneous, isotropic, linear, nondispersive medium with time-independent scalar \(\varepsilon,\mu\). Hence \(\mathbf D=\varepsilon\mathbf E\) and \(\mathbf B=\mu\mathbf H\).

Electric-field wave equation · every major step

Start from Faraday's law and take a curl:

\[\nabla\times\mathbf E=-\mu\frac{\partial\mathbf H}{\partial t},\] \[\nabla\times(\nabla\times\mathbf E) =-\mu\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(\nabla\times\mathbf H).\]

Use source-free Ampère–Maxwell: \(\nabla\times\mathbf H=\varepsilon\,\partial_t\mathbf E\):

\[\nabla\times(\nabla\times\mathbf E)=-\mu\varepsilon\frac{\partial^2\mathbf E}{\partial t^2}.\]

Now apply the vector identity:

\[\nabla\times(\nabla\times\mathbf E)=\nabla(\nabla\cdot\mathbf E)-\nabla^2\mathbf E.\]

Since \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf D=0\) and \(\varepsilon\) is constant, \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf E=0\), so

\[-\nabla^2\mathbf E=-\mu\varepsilon\frac{\partial^2\mathbf E}{\partial t^2}.\] \[\boxed{\nabla^2\mathbf E=\mu\varepsilon\frac{\partial^2\mathbf E}{\partial t^2}}.\]

This is a wave equation with propagation speed

\[\boxed{v=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu\varepsilon}}}.\]

In vacuum, \(v=c=1/\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0}\).

Magnetic-field wave equation

Start from \(\nabla\times\mathbf H=\varepsilon\,\partial_t\mathbf E\) and curl both sides:

\[\nabla\times(\nabla\times\mathbf H)=\varepsilon\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(\nabla\times\mathbf E).\]

Substitute Faraday's law \(\nabla\times\mathbf E=-\mu\,\partial_t\mathbf H\), and use \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf H=0\) (since \(\mathbf B=\mu\mathbf H\) and constant \(\mu\)):

\[-\nabla^2\mathbf H=-\mu\varepsilon\,\partial_t^2\mathbf H.\] \[\boxed{\nabla^2\mathbf H=\mu\varepsilon\frac{\partial^2\mathbf H}{\partial t^2}}.\]

Because \(\mu\) is constant, \(\mathbf B\) obeys the corresponding wave equation. These simple forms generally fail when material properties vary spatially or are dispersive.

Explicit equations for electric and magnetic flux density

The derivations above give a wave equation for \(\mathbf E\) and \(\mathbf H\). Because \(\mathbf B=\mu\mathbf H\) and \(\mu\) is constant, the magnetic flux density obeys the same form:

\[\boxed{\nabla^2\mathbf E-\mu\varepsilon\frac{\partial^2\mathbf E}{\partial t^2}=\mathbf0}\] \[\boxed{\nabla^2\mathbf B-\mu\varepsilon\frac{\partial^2\mathbf B}{\partial t^2}=\mathbf0}.\]

In vacuum substitute \(\mu_0,\varepsilon_0\) and use \(\boxed{c=1/\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0}}\). In a source-free homogeneous lossless medium \(\boxed{v=1/\sqrt{\mu\varepsilon}}\).

Derive the wave equation directly for \(\mathbf B\)

From the homogeneous source-free equations, \(\nabla\times\mathbf B=\mu\varepsilon\,\partial_t\mathbf E\) and \(\nabla\times\mathbf E=-\partial_t\mathbf B\). Take the curl of Ampère–Maxwell:

\[\nabla\times(\nabla\times\mathbf B)= \mu\varepsilon\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(\nabla\times\mathbf E).\]

Insert Faraday's law and the curl–curl identity:

\[\nabla(\nabla\cdot\mathbf B)-\nabla^2\mathbf B =-\mu\varepsilon\,\frac{\partial^2\mathbf B}{\partial t^2}.\]

By magnetic Gauss's law \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0\), so

\[\boxed{\nabla^2\mathbf B-\mu\varepsilon\,\partial_t^2\mathbf B=\mathbf0}.\]
Conducting materials: damped wave equations

For constant \(\sigma,\varepsilon,\mu\), Ohm's law \(\mathbf J_f=\sigma\mathbf E\), and \(\rho_f=0\), Ampère–Maxwell becomes

\[\nabla\times\mathbf B=\mu\sigma\mathbf E+\mu\varepsilon\,\partial_t\mathbf E.\]

Curl Faraday's law, substitute this expression, and use \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf E=0\). For the magnetic equation, instead curl the expression above and use Faraday's law and \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0\):

\[\boxed{\nabla^2\mathbf E-\mu\varepsilon\,\partial_t^2\mathbf E-\mu\sigma\,\partial_t\mathbf E=\mathbf0},\] \[\boxed{\nabla^2\mathbf B-\mu\varepsilon\,\partial_t^2\mathbf B-\mu\sigma\,\partial_t\mathbf B=\mathbf0}.\]

The extra first-time-derivative term represents dissipative electromagnetic attenuation in an Ohmic conductor. These simple constant-coefficient forms do not apply universally to dispersive, inhomogeneous or anisotropic matter.

03 / HELMHOLTZ EQUATIONS

Derive the Helmholtz equations for \(\mathbf E\) and \(\mathbf B\)

The wave equations describe fields changing in space and time. For a fixed angular frequency \(\omega\), write the known time dependence separately; the remaining spatial fields satisfy a Helmholtz equation.

Define the phasors

\[\boxed{\mathbf E(\mathbf r,t)=\operatorname{Re} \{\widetilde{\mathbf E}(\mathbf r)e^{-i\omega t}\}}\] \[\boxed{\mathbf B(\mathbf r,t)=\operatorname{Re} \{\widetilde{\mathbf B}(\mathbf r)e^{-i\omega t}\}}\]

The tildes are complex spatial amplitudes, not different physical fields. With the chosen \(e^{-i\omega t}\) convention, \(\partial_t\to-i\omega\) and \(\partial_t^2\to-\omega^2\).

Electric-field Helmholtz equation: all steps

Start from the homogeneous source-free wave equation

\[\nabla^2\mathbf E-\mu\varepsilon\frac{\partial^2\mathbf E}{\partial t^2}=\mathbf0.\]

Substitute \(\widetilde{\mathbf E}e^{-i\omega t}\) and evaluate the time derivatives:

\[\nabla^2\widetilde{\mathbf E}\,e^{-i\omega t} +\mu\varepsilon\omega^2\widetilde{\mathbf E}\,e^{-i\omega t}=\mathbf0.\]

Divide by the nonzero harmonic factor:

\[\nabla^2\widetilde{\mathbf E}+\omega^2\mu\varepsilon\,\widetilde{\mathbf E}=\mathbf0.\]

Define \(k^2=\omega^2\mu\varepsilon\), giving

\[\boxed{(\nabla^2+k^2)\widetilde{\mathbf E}=\mathbf0}.\]
Magnetic-field Helmholtz equation: all steps

Start from \(\nabla^2\mathbf B-\mu\varepsilon\,\partial_t^2\mathbf B=\mathbf0\). Substitute \(\mathbf B\propto\widetilde{\mathbf B}e^{-i\omega t}\):

\[\nabla^2\widetilde{\mathbf B}\,e^{-i\omega t} +\omega^2\mu\varepsilon\,\widetilde{\mathbf B}\,e^{-i\omega t}=\mathbf0.\]

Remove the time factor:

\[\boxed{(\nabla^2+k^2)\widetilde{\mathbf B}=\mathbf0}.\]

Both fields share \(k=\omega\sqrt{\mu\varepsilon}=\omega/v\) in this uniform lossless medium. In vacuum, \(k_0=\omega/c\). The Helmholtz equations must be used with Maxwell's divergence and curl constraints; arbitrary independent vector solutions are not necessarily electromagnetic waves.

Solve the 1D Helmholtz equation

If the wave propagates along \(z\) and the electric field points along \(x\), use \(\widetilde{\mathbf E}(z)=\widetilde E_x(z)\hat{\mathbf x}\). Then

\[\frac{d^2\widetilde E_x}{dz^2}+k^2\widetilde E_x=0.\]

The characteristic equation is \(r^2+k^2=0\), so \(r=\pm ik\) and

\[\boxed{\widetilde E_x(z)=Ae^{ikz}+Be^{-ikz}}.\]

For forward propagation, take the real part of \(E_0e^{i(kz-\omega t)}\), obtaining

\[\boxed{\mathbf E(z,t)=E_0\cos(kz-\omega t)\hat{\mathbf x}}.\]

Faraday's law implies \(\widetilde{\mathbf B}=(\mathbf k\times\widetilde{\mathbf E})/\omega\) for a plane wave. Hence when \(\mathbf k\parallel\hat{\mathbf z}\) and \(\mathbf E\parallel\hat{\mathbf x}\), the magnetic field points along \(+\hat{\mathbf y}\). The field component's direction and the derivative's coordinate are not the same thing.

Conducting medium: complex Helmholtz wavenumber

In the Ohmic model above, the first-time derivative becomes \(-i\omega\widetilde{\mathbf E}\) or \(-i\omega\widetilde{\mathbf B}\), and the two Helmholtz equations become

\[\boxed{(\nabla^2+k_c^2)\widetilde{\mathbf E}=\mathbf0},\qquad \boxed{(\nabla^2+k_c^2)\widetilde{\mathbf B}=\mathbf0},\] \[\boxed{k_c^2=\omega^2\mu\varepsilon+i\omega\mu\sigma}.\]

For forward waves with spatial dependence \(e^{ik_cz}\), choose the root with \(\operatorname{Im}k_c>0\) in a passive material, producing exponential attenuation. Material loss therefore modifies the Helmholtz wavenumber.

Helmholtz versus electrostatic Laplace and Poisson

Electrostatic scalar potential satisfies

\[\nabla^2V=0\quad(\rho=0),\qquad \nabla^2V=-\frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0}\quad(\text{vacuum Poisson}).\]

Time-harmonic source-free vector fields satisfy instead

\[(\nabla^2+k^2)\widetilde{\mathbf E}=\mathbf0,\qquad (\nabla^2+k^2)\widetilde{\mathbf B}=\mathbf0.\]

The nonzero \(k^2\) term encodes spatial oscillations at angular frequency \(\omega\). The static-potential equations and wave equations are physically different boundary-value problems.

04 / PLANE WAVES

Wave direction versus field direction

Plane wave traveling in \(+\hat{\mathbf z}\)

\[\mathbf E(z,t)=E_0\cos(kz-\omega t)\,\hat{\mathbf x}\] \[\mathbf B(z,t)=\frac{E_0}{v}\cos(kz-\omega t)\,\hat{\mathbf y}\]

The field points along \(x\), while the wave propagates along \(z\). Differentiating \(E_x\) with respect to \(z\) is therefore completely consistent.

Derive B, wave speed and wave impedance

Represent a monochromatic wave using a complex amplitude, taking the real part at the end:

\[\mathbf E=\operatorname{Re}\{\mathbf E_0e^{i(\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r-\omega t)}\}.\]

For this wave, \(\nabla\times\mathbf E\leftrightarrow i\mathbf k\times\mathbf E_0\) and \(\partial_t\mathbf B\leftrightarrow-i\omega\mathbf B_0\). Faraday's law gives

\[i\mathbf k\times\mathbf E_0=i\omega\mathbf B_0,\] \[\boxed{\mathbf B_0=\frac{1}{\omega}\mathbf k\times\mathbf E_0}.\]

For propagation along \(+\hat{\mathbf z}\), \(\mathbf k=k\hat{\mathbf z}\), and polarization \(\mathbf E_0=E_0\hat{\mathbf x}\):

\[\mathbf B_0=\frac{kE_0}{\omega}(\hat{\mathbf z}\times\hat{\mathbf x})=\frac{E_0}{v}\hat{\mathbf y}.\]

Here \(v=\omega/k\). Because \(\mathbf B=\mu\mathbf H\),

\[\boxed{\mathbf H=\frac{1}{\eta}\hat{\mathbf z}\times\mathbf E},\qquad \boxed{\eta=\sqrt{\frac{\mu}{\varepsilon}}}.\]

\(\eta\) is the wave impedance. The fields \(\mathbf E\), \(\mathbf B\), and propagation direction form a right-handed orthogonal triad in this isotropic medium.

05 / ENERGY STORAGE AND TRANSPORT

Derive Poynting's theorem

In a linear, isotropic, nondispersive medium with time-independent \(\varepsilon\) and \(\mu\), electromagnetic energy density and flux have particularly simple forms.

From Maxwell's equations to energy conservation

Start with the vector identity

\[\nabla\cdot(\mathbf E\times\mathbf H) =\mathbf H\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf E)-\mathbf E\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf H).\]

Insert Faraday's and Ampère–Maxwell's laws:

\[\nabla\cdot(\mathbf E\times\mathbf H) =-\mathbf H\cdot\frac{\partial\mathbf B}{\partial t} -\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf J_f -\mathbf E\cdot\frac{\partial\mathbf D}{\partial t}.\]

With \(\mathbf B=\mu\mathbf H\), \(\mathbf D=\varepsilon\mathbf E\), and constant \(\varepsilon,\mu\),

\[\mathbf H\cdot\partial_t\mathbf B=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\frac{\mu H^2}{2}\right),\qquad \mathbf E\cdot\partial_t\mathbf D=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\frac{\varepsilon E^2}{2}\right).\]

Move energy-derivative terms to the left:

\[\boxed{\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot\mathbf S=-\mathbf J_f\cdot\mathbf E},\] \[\boxed{u=\frac12\varepsilon E^2+\frac12\mu H^2},\qquad \boxed{\mathbf S=\mathbf E\times\mathbf H}.\]

Meaning: a region loses field energy by transporting it outward (\(\nabla\cdot\mathbf S>0\)) or by doing work on free charges (\(\mathbf J_f\cdot\mathbf E>0\)). For dispersive or lossy matter the simple expression for \(u\) need not apply.

Energy stored per unit volume

\[\boxed{u_{\mathrm{vac}}=\frac{\varepsilon_0E^2}{2}+\frac{B^2}{2\mu_0}}\]

The electric and magnetic contributions are \(u_E=\varepsilon_0E^2/2\) and \(u_B=B^2/(2\mu_0)\). Units: \(\mathrm{J/m^3}\). These energy densities exist even for static fields.

In a uniform, linear, lossless nondispersive material,

\[\boxed{u=\frac12\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf D+\frac12\mathbf B\cdot\mathbf H =\frac{\varepsilon E^2}{2}+\frac{B^2}{2\mu}}.\]

In dispersive, nonlinear, hysteretic or dissipative media, this simple formula is not generally sufficient to describe all stored material and electromagnetic energy.

Energy transferred per area per time

\[\boxed{\mathbf S=\mathbf E\times\mathbf H},\qquad \boxed{\mathbf S_{\mathrm{vac}}=\frac1{\mu_0}\mathbf E\times\mathbf B}.\]

The Poynting vector has units \(\mathrm{W/m^2}\), not \(\mathrm{J/m^3}\). Its direction gives the energy-flow direction in an ordinary traveling plane wave. A positive divergence means net outward flow from a small volume.

Convert Poynting's theorem to an integral law

Starting from the differential theorem derived above,

\[\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot\mathbf S=-\mathbf J_f\cdot\mathbf E,\]

integrate over a fixed volume \(\mathcal V\), exchange the time derivative and volume integral, and apply the divergence theorem:

\[\int_{\mathcal V}\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}\,dV +\int_{\mathcal V}\nabla\cdot\mathbf S\,dV =-\int_{\mathcal V}\mathbf J_f\cdot\mathbf E\,dV,\] \[\boxed{\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\mathcal V}u\,dV =-\oint_{\partial\mathcal V}\mathbf S\cdot d\mathbf a -\int_{\mathcal V}\mathbf J_f\cdot\mathbf E\,dV}.\]

Field energy inside decreases if electromagnetic power flows outward or if fields do positive work on charges. For Ohmic conduction, \(\mathbf J_f\cdot\mathbf E=\sigma E^2\) is the local rate of Joule heating per volume.

Worked example: energy density and intensity of a plane wave

For an ideal lossless plane wave in a homogeneous isotropic medium, let

\[\mathbf E=E_0\cos(kz-\omega t)\hat{\mathbf x},\qquad \mathbf H=\frac{E_0}{\eta}\cos(kz-\omega t)\hat{\mathbf y},\]

with impedance \(\eta=\sqrt{\mu/\varepsilon}\) and speed \(v=1/\sqrt{\mu\varepsilon}\). Since \(B=\mu H=E/v\), the energy contributions are equal:

\[u_E=\frac{\varepsilon E^2}{2},\qquad u_B=\frac{B^2}{2\mu}=\frac{\varepsilon E^2}{2}.\]

Add them:

\[\boxed{u=\varepsilon E_0^2\cos^2(kz-\omega t)}.\]

Compute the energy-flux vector:

\[\boxed{\mathbf S=\frac{E_0^2}{\eta}\cos^2(kz-\omega t)\hat{\mathbf z}}.\]

Average over a period using \(\langle\cos^2\rangle=1/2\):

\[\boxed{\langle u\rangle=\frac12\varepsilon E_0^2},\qquad \boxed{\langle\mathbf S\rangle=\frac{E_0^2}{2\eta}\hat{\mathbf z}}.\]

Since \(1/\eta=\varepsilon v\), the magnitude obeys \(\boxed{\langle S\rangle=v\langle u\rangle}\). This connects energy per unit volume with the rate of its transport.

Harmonic phasors: time-averaged Poynting vector

For fields represented by complex phasors under the \(e^{-i\omega t}\) convention, the time average is

\[\boxed{\langle\mathbf S\rangle=\frac12\operatorname{Re} \left(\widetilde{\mathbf E}\times\widetilde{\mathbf H}^{\,*}\right)}.\]

The star denotes complex conjugation. This formula is useful when field phasors come from solutions to Helmholtz equations.

06 / WAVES AT AN INTERFACE

Reflection and transmission at normal incidence

Consider a plane wave traveling from a lossless isotropic medium 1 into medium 2 across the plane \(z=0\), with no free surface charge or current. Their wave impedances are \(\eta_1,\eta_2\). At normal incidence, tangential \(\mathbf E\) and \(\mathbf H\) are continuous.

Derive electric-field reflection and transmission coefficients

Let \(E_i\), \(E_r\), \(E_t\) be complex phasor amplitudes of the incident, reflected and transmitted electric fields, all along \(\hat{\mathbf x}\). Then \(\mathbf H\) for a \(+\hat{\mathbf z}\)-traveling wave is \(+\hat{\mathbf y}E/\eta\), while the reflected wave travels in \(-\hat{\mathbf z}\) and therefore has \(\mathbf H_r=-\hat{\mathbf y}E_r/\eta_1\).

The two interface boundary conditions are

\[E_i+E_r=E_t,\qquad\frac{E_i-E_r}{\eta_1}=\frac{E_t}{\eta_2}.\]

Define \(r=E_r/E_i\) and \(t=E_t/E_i\):

\[1+r=t,\qquad\frac{1-r}{\eta_1}=\frac{1+r}{\eta_2}.\]

Multiply through and solve:

\[\eta_2-\eta_2r=\eta_1+\eta_1r,\] \[\boxed{r=\frac{\eta_2-\eta_1}{\eta_2+\eta_1}},\qquad \boxed{t=\frac{2\eta_2}{\eta_1+\eta_2}}.\]

For real positive impedances and lossless materials, time-averaged power reflection and transmission are

\[\boxed{R=|r|^2},\qquad \boxed{T=\frac{\eta_1}{\eta_2}|t|^2},\qquad R+T=1.\]

Meaning: impedance mismatch generates reflection. The sign of \(r\) indicates whether the reflected electric field reverses its phase. Equal impedances give \(r=0\), even if the wave speeds differ.

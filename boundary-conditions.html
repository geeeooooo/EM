Boundary Conditions

What a boundary condition tells us. It determines which field components can change discontinuously across the interface of two media, and what causes those changes. These rules come directly from the integral forms of Maxwell's equations.

Geometry: the interface is \(z=0\). Medium 1 occupies \(z<0\); medium 2 occupies \(z>0\). The normal \(\hat{\mathbf n}=\hat{\mathbf z}\) points from 1 toward 2. The limiting values of each field are \(\mathbf F_1\) and \(\mathbf F_2\). Free surface charge and current densities are \(\sigma_f\) and \(\mathbf K_f\).

SI units and physical constants

Fields: \(\mathbf E\): electric field, \(\mathrm{V/m}=\mathrm{N/C}\); \(\mathbf D\): displacement, \(\mathrm{C/m^2}\); \(\mathbf B\): flux density, tesla \(\mathrm{T}=\mathrm{Wb/m^2}\); \(\mathbf H\): field intensity, \(\mathrm{A/m}\); \(V\): electric potential, volts \(\mathrm{V}\).

Sources: \(Q\): charge, coulombs \(\mathrm{C}\); \(\rho,\rho_f\): volume charge density, \(\mathrm{C/m^3}\); \(\sigma_f\): surface charge density, \(\mathrm{C/m^2}\); \(\mathbf J,\mathbf J_f\): current density, \(\mathrm{A/m^2}\); \(\mathbf K_f\): surface current density, \(\mathrm{A/m}\).

Materials: \(\varepsilon,\varepsilon_0\): permittivity, \(\mathrm{F/m}\); \(\mu,\mu_0\): permeability, \(\mathrm{H/m}\); \(\mathbf P\): polarization, \(\mathrm{C/m^2}\); \(\mathbf M\): magnetization, \(\mathrm{A/m}\); \(\sigma\): conductivity, \(\mathrm{S/m}\); \(\eta\): wave impedance, ohms \(\Omega\). Relative permittivity, relative permeability and refractive index \(n\) are dimensionless.

Vacuum constants:

\[\varepsilon_0\approx8.854\times10^{-12}\ \mathrm{F/m},\quad \mu_0\approx1.257\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{H/m}\] \[c=299\,792\,458\ \mathrm{m/s},\quad \eta_0\approx377\ \Omega\]

The vacuum speed of light \(c\) is exact by SI definition; the other displayed values are rounded.

Space and waves: lengths \(\mathrm{m}\), area \(\mathrm{m^2}\), time \(\mathrm{s}\); \(\omega\): angular frequency, \(\mathrm{rad/s}\); \(k\): wavenumber, \(\mathrm{rad/m}\); \(v\): propagation speed, \(\mathrm{m/s}\). Angles in radians are dimensionless in SI. Logarithms and exponentials must have dimensionless arguments.

Energy: energy density \(u\): \(\mathrm{J/m^3}\); Poynting vector \(\mathbf S\): \(\mathrm{W/m^2}\); power delivered to charges per volume \(\mathbf J\cdot\mathbf E\): \(\mathrm{W/m^3}\). Magnetic flux \(\Phi_B\): webers \(\mathrm{Wb}\); displacement flux \(\Phi_D\): coulombs \(\mathrm{C}\).

01 / FOUR INTERFACE CONDITIONS

At a glance

Units: \(D_n,\sigma_f\) in \(\mathrm{C/m^2}\); \(B_n\) in \(\mathrm{T}\); \(E_t\) in \(\mathrm{V/m}\); \(H_t,K_f\) in \(\mathrm{A/m}\). A unit normal is dimensionless.

Principle before equations: what is continuous at an interface?

Statement. Maxwell's boundary conditions determine how the normal and tangential components of the fields change when crossing a material interface. Free surface charge permits a jump in normal \(\mathbf D\); a free surface current permits a jump in tangential \(\mathbf H\); normal \(\mathbf B\) and tangential \(\mathbf E\) remain continuous for an ordinary interface.

Physical meaning. A field component is constrained by the way its associated flux or circulation encounters an infinitesimally thin interface. Surface sources appear as finite terms when the Gaussian pillbox or Amperian loop shrinks to zero thickness.

Assumptions. Take \(\hat{\mathbf n}\) from medium 1 to medium 2. The usual conditions assume an ordinary stationary interface with fields finite enough that area terms vanish as the loop shrinks; singular idealized sheets are represented by \(\sigma_f,\mathbf K_f\). Snell's law is a related *wave-direction* law, not the same as these four field-component conditions.

Normal components · Gaussian pillbox

\[\boxed{\hat{\mathbf n}\cdot(\mathbf D_2-\mathbf D_1)=\sigma_f}\] \[\boxed{\hat{\mathbf n}\cdot(\mathbf B_2-\mathbf B_1)=0}\]

Normal \(\mathbf D\) may jump because of free surface charge; normal \(\mathbf B\) is continuous because there are no magnetic monopoles in classical theory.

Tangential components · Thin rectangular loop

\[\boxed{\hat{\mathbf n}\times(\mathbf E_2-\mathbf E_1)=\mathbf 0}\] \[\boxed{\hat{\mathbf n}\times(\mathbf H_2-\mathbf H_1)=\mathbf K_f}\]

Tangential \(\mathbf E\) is continuous for ordinary finite electromagnetic fields; a free surface-current sheet can create a jump in tangential \(\mathbf H\).

These are macroscopic SI boundary conditions for stationary interfaces with regular finite fields (aside from the stated surface charges/currents). They do not presume the media to be linear or isotropic.

02 / GAUSSIAN PILLBOX

Derive the normal conditions

Geometry: pillbox area \(A\) in \(\mathrm{m^2}\), height \(h\) in \(\mathrm{m}\), enclosed charge \(Q_{f,\mathrm{enc}}\) in \(\mathrm{C}\). Hence \(D_n A\) has units \(\mathrm{C}\).

Why a pillbox gives the normal conditions

Statement. Gauss's law constrains the total flux of \(\mathbf D\) and \(\mathbf B\) through a closed surface. A thin pillbox intersects the interface with two oppositely oriented faces, isolating the difference between the fields' normal components.

Physical meaning. As the pillbox thickness tends to zero, the side-wall area and its contribution to flux vanish if the fields stay finite. The free sheet charge remains finite because it lies on the interface. For \(\mathbf B\), no magnetic monopole source exists in Maxwell theory, so its normal flux cannot jump.

Assumptions. The interface is locally smooth, a finite surface charge density may exist, and the normal orientation is fixed. The pillbox does not directly determine tangential components, nor does it imply \(\mathbf E_n\) is continuous across dielectrics with different permittivities.

Construct the pillbox

Choose a cylinder of cross-sectional area \(A\), with thickness \(h\), crossing \(z=0\). Its top face is in medium 2 and has outward normal \(+\hat{\mathbf n}\). Its bottom face is in medium 1 and has outward normal \(-\hat{\mathbf n}\).

When \(h\to 0\), the lateral area is proportional to \(h\); its flux tends to zero for bounded fields. The top and bottom fluxes remain finite.

Electric condition · full derivation from Gauss's law

Begin with the integral macroscopic Gauss law:

\[\oint_{\partial\mathcal V}\mathbf D\cdot d\mathbf a=Q_{f,\mathrm{enc}}.\]

Split the closed-surface integral into top, bottom and side contributions:

\[\mathbf D_2\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}\,A-\mathbf D_1\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}\,A+\Phi_{\mathrm{side}}=Q_{f,\mathrm{enc}}.\]

As \(h\to0\): \(\Phi_{\mathrm{side}}\to0\), the regular volume free charge contributes \(O(h)\), but a sheet of free charge contributes \(\sigma_f A\).

\[(D_{2n}-D_{1n})A=\sigma_f A.\]

Divide by \(A\):

\[\boxed{D_{2n}-D_{1n}=\sigma_f}.\]

Meaning: free surface charge fixes the jump in the component of \(\mathbf D\) normal to the interface. If \(\sigma_f=0\), \(D_{2n}=D_{1n}\), even if the individual electric-field components differ.

Magnetic condition · full derivation from magnetic Gauss law
\[\oint_{\partial\mathcal V}\mathbf B\cdot d\mathbf a=0.\] \[\mathbf B_2\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}\,A-\mathbf B_1\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}\,A+\Phi_{\mathrm{side}}=0.\]

For \(h\to0\), \(\Phi_{\mathrm{side}}\to0\), hence

\[(B_{2n}-B_{1n})A=0\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad\boxed{B_{2n}=B_{1n}}.\]

Meaning: a material interface cannot terminate the normal magnetic flux. This is about \(\mathbf B\); it does not require continuity of \(H_n\).

03 / AMPERIAN AND FARADAY LOOPS

Derive the tangential conditions

Loop units: \(\ell,h\) in \(\mathrm{m}\), \(\oint\mathbf E\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell\) in \(\mathrm{V}\), \(\oint\mathbf H\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell\) and \(I_f\) in amperes \(\mathrm{A}\).

Why a vanishing loop gives tangential conditions

Statement. Faraday's law relates electric circulation to changing magnetic flux; Ampère–Maxwell relates magnetic circulation to enclosed free current and changing displacement flux. A narrow rectangular loop crossing the interface isolates the fields' tangential components on its long sides.

Physical meaning. As the loop height goes to zero, its enclosed area vanishes, so an ordinary finite time derivative of flux contributes nothing. A free sheet current crossing the loop remains finite and accounts for the tangential jump of \(\mathbf H\).

Conditions. Specify the loop and surface orientations using the right-hand rule. The standard continuity of \(\mathbf E_t\) assumes no singular magnetic sheet flux changing in time. The full vector cross-product conditions encode results for every tangential direction, not just the direction chosen for one loop.

Construct the loop

Take a rectangular loop with length \(\ell\) parallel to any tangential unit vector \(\hat{\mathbf t}\) and height \(h\) across the interface. Orient its upper edge in \(+\hat{\mathbf t}\), its lower edge in \(-\hat{\mathbf t}\), and its area normal as \(\hat{\mathbf n}\times\hat{\mathbf t}\).

As \(h\to0\), short-side circulations vanish. For bounded fields and finite time derivatives, flux through the shrinking rectangle also vanishes. A free surface current, however, gives a finite current crossing the rectangle.

Faraday's law → continuity of tangential E

Start from Faraday's law on a fixed loop:

\[\oint_{\partial\mathcal S}\mathbf E\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell=-\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\mathcal S}\mathbf B\cdot d\mathbf a.\]

The upper and lower segments produce

\[\oint_{\partial\mathcal S}\mathbf E\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell=(\mathbf E_2-\mathbf E_1)\cdot\hat{\mathbf t}\,\ell+O(h).\]

The magnetic flux is proportional to \(\ell h\), so its time derivative tends to zero as \(h\to0\) under the stated regularity assumptions.

\[(\mathbf E_2-\mathbf E_1)\cdot\hat{\mathbf t}\,\ell=0.\]

Since \(\ell\ne0\) and \(\hat{\mathbf t}\) can be any tangent direction,

\[\boxed{\mathbf E_{2t}=\mathbf E_{1t}} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \boxed{\hat{\mathbf n}\times(\mathbf E_2-\mathbf E_1)=\mathbf0}.\]

Meaning: the electric field cannot have an ordinary tangential jump; a time-varying \(\mathbf B\) still produces a circulating field over finite loops.

Ampère–Maxwell law → tangential H jump

Begin with

\[\oint_{\partial\mathcal S}\mathbf H\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell=I_{f,\mathrm{enc}}+\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\mathcal S}\mathbf D\cdot d\mathbf a.\]

The thin-loop circulation is

\[\oint_{\partial\mathcal S}\mathbf H\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell=(\mathbf H_2-\mathbf H_1)\cdot\hat{\mathbf t}\,\ell+O(h).\]

The regular displacement-flux term vanishes as \(h\to0\); a free surface current remains:

\[I_{f,\mathrm{enc}}\ \longrightarrow\ \mathbf K_f\cdot(\hat{\mathbf n}\times\hat{\mathbf t})\,\ell.\]

Therefore

\[(\mathbf H_2-\mathbf H_1)\cdot\hat{\mathbf t}=\mathbf K_f\cdot(\hat{\mathbf n}\times\hat{\mathbf t}).\]

This must hold for every tangent direction. Equivalently,

\[\boxed{\hat{\mathbf n}\times(\mathbf H_2-\mathbf H_1)=\mathbf K_f}.\]

Meaning: a sheet of free current makes the tangential magnetic field intensity discontinuous. The orientation of the cross product matters; reversing \(\hat{\mathbf n}\) reverses the jump convention.

04 / ELECTROSTATIC INTERFACES

Apply the boundary conditions to electrostatics

Potential and derivatives: \(V\) in volts \(\mathrm{V}\), \(\partial V/\partial n\) in \(\mathrm{V/m}\), and \(\varepsilon\,\partial V/\partial n\) in \(\mathrm{C/m^2}\), the same unit as \(\sigma_f\).

Electrostatic interface principle

Statement. In electrostatic equilibrium, \(\nabla\times\mathbf E=0\), so \(\mathbf E=-\nabla V\) in a simply connected region. Continuity of tangential \(\mathbf E\) ordinarily implies continuity of scalar potential \(V\) across a regular interface; normal \(\mathbf D\) changes only according to free surface charge.

Physical meaning. The same voltage must be approached from either side of an ordinary dielectric boundary, but the normal electric field may differ because the media polarize differently.

Assumptions and exceptions. Ordinary finite fields and no ideal dipole layer are assumed for continuity of \(V\). A perfect conductor in electrostatic equilibrium is equipotential and has vanishing interior \(\mathbf E\). These are static statements; do not automatically use them for a time-dependent conductor.

In electrostatics, \(\partial\mathbf B/\partial t=0\), so \(\nabla\times\mathbf E=0\) and \(\mathbf E=-\nabla V\).

Potential at a regular dielectric interface

\[\boxed{V_2=V_1},\qquad\boxed{D_{2n}-D_{1n}=\sigma_f}.\]

Potential is continuous for an ordinary interface with finite field and no idealized dipole sheet. This is distinct from its normal derivative, which generally changes.

Derive the normal-derivative boundary condition

Suppose both media are linear, isotropic dielectrics with local permittivities \(\varepsilon_1,\varepsilon_2\). Then \(\mathbf D_i=\varepsilon_i\mathbf E_i\) and \(\mathbf E_i=-\nabla V_i\).

\[D_{2n}-D_{1n}=\sigma_f\] \[-\varepsilon_2\frac{\partial V_2}{\partial n}+\varepsilon_1\frac{\partial V_1}{\partial n}=\sigma_f.\] \[\boxed{\varepsilon_2\frac{\partial V_2}{\partial n}-\varepsilon_1\frac{\partial V_1}{\partial n}=-\sigma_f}.\]

For \(\sigma_f=0\), the weighted normal derivatives match, although \(\partial_n V_2\) and \(\partial_n V_1\) need not be equal.

Conductor in electrostatic equilibrium

Within an ideal conductor at electrostatic equilibrium, \(\mathbf E_{\mathrm{inside}}=0\). Faraday's loop condition gives \(\mathbf E_{\mathrm{outside},t}=0\), so the field just outside is normal to the surface.

The pillbox condition becomes \(D_{\mathrm{outside},n}=\sigma_f\) when \(\hat{\mathbf n}\) points from conductor to exterior. For an isotropic surrounding dielectric,

\[\boxed{\mathbf E_{\mathrm{outside}}=\frac{\sigma_f}{\varepsilon}\hat{\mathbf n}}.\]

The conductor surface is equipotential. Charges may still reside on the surface, creating a normal electric field outside.

Worked example · dielectric interface with no free surface charge

Let the interface be \(z=0\), with \(\varepsilon_2=2\varepsilon_1\), \(\sigma_f=0\), and immediately below the interface

\[\mathbf E_1=(10\hat{\mathbf x}+30\hat{\mathbf z})\ \mathrm{V/m}.\]

Tangential continuity gives \(E_{2x}=E_{1x}=10\ \mathrm{V/m}\).

Normal \(\mathbf D\) continuity gives \(\varepsilon_2E_{2z}=\varepsilon_1E_{1z}\):

\[E_{2z}=\frac{\varepsilon_1}{2\varepsilon_1}(30)=15\ \mathrm{V/m}.\] \[\boxed{\mathbf E_2=(10\hat{\mathbf x}+15\hat{\mathbf z})\ \mathrm{V/m}}.\]

Concept: the tangential electric field is unchanged while its normal component is reduced in the medium of higher permittivity.

Find how the electric field line bends

Let \(\theta_j\) be the angle between the static electric field \(\mathbf E_j\) and the interface normal \(+\hat{\mathbf z}\). In this example both fields point toward \(+\hat{\mathbf z}\), so \(\tan\theta_j=|E_{jt}|/E_{jn}\), where \(E_{jn}=E_{jz}\) and \(|E_{jt}|=|E_{jx}|\).

For linear isotropic dielectrics with no free surface charge, \(E_{2t}=E_{1t}\) and \(E_{2n}=(\varepsilon_1/\varepsilon_2)E_{1n}\). For fields crossing the interface in the direction of the normal, these give

\[\tan\theta_2=\frac{|E_{1t}|}{(\varepsilon_1/\varepsilon_2)E_{1n}}\] \[\boxed{\tan\theta_2=\frac{\varepsilon_2}{\varepsilon_1}\tan\theta_1}.\] \[\theta_1=\arctan\!\left(\frac{10}{30}\right)\approx18.4^\circ,\] \[\theta_2=\arctan\!\left(\frac{10}{15}\right)\approx33.7^\circ.\]

The static field line bends away from the normal as it enters medium 2. The tangential component stays the same while the normal component decreases, so the angle to the normal increases.

Here the angle describes the direction of the static electric field. In optical Snell's law, the angle describes the direction of the wavevector.

05 / DYNAMIC FIELDS AND MAGNETIC INTERFACES

Boundary conditions in electromagnetics

Wave constants: \(\varepsilon\) in \(\mathrm{F/m}\), \(\mu\) in \(\mathrm{H/m}\), impedance \(\eta=\sqrt{\mu/\varepsilon}\) in ohms \(\Omega\). Amplitude reflection and transmission coefficients are dimensionless.

Principle before proof: interfaces carrying waves

Statement. The same Maxwell interface conditions govern time-varying fields. For a wave striking a stationary interface, tangential fields must match at every position and time. This produces reflected and transmitted waves whose amplitudes and phases are constrained by the interface conditions.

Physical meaning. Different impedances require a reflected wave so that the sum of incident and reflected tangential fields is consistent with the transmitted field. The direction of transmitted propagation follows phase matching; its amplitude follows the field-component equations.

Conditions. Simple Fresnel coefficients assume planar uniform interfaces and specified linear homogeneous media, often lossless isotropic media at normal incidence. Material conductivity, anisotropy, surface currents, and oblique incidence may change the details. Distinguish impedance from refractive index.

The same four macroscopic conditions apply to time-varying electromagnetic fields under the ordinary regularity assumptions, because the shrinking pillbox and loop make finite volume and displacement-flux terms vanish.

Be careful about which fields are continuous

With no free surface charge or current, \(D_n\), \(B_n\), \(E_t\), and \(H_t\) are continuous. It does not follow that all components of \(\mathbf E\), \(\mathbf D\), \(\mathbf B\), or \(\mathbf H\) are continuous. For linear isotropic materials, \(D_n=\varepsilon E_n\) and \(B_n=\mu H_n\) explain why normal \(E\) and normal \(H\) can change.

Special case · magnetostatic material boundary

For static fields, \(\partial_t\mathbf D=0\), so

\[\nabla\times\mathbf H=\mathbf J_f.\]

The thin-loop derivation is otherwise unchanged, giving

\[\boxed{\hat{\mathbf n}\times(\mathbf H_2-\mathbf H_1)=\mathbf K_f}.\]

The magnetic Gauss pillbox still gives \(B_{2n}=B_{1n}\). For isotropic linear media with no sheet current,

\[\boxed{\frac{B_{2t}}{\mu_2}=\frac{B_{1t}}{\mu_1}},\qquad\boxed{B_{2n}=B_{1n}}.\]

This shows why magnetic flux density \(\mathbf B\) is not generally continuous in all components at an interface.

Normal-incidence plane waves · reflected and transmitted fields

For two lossless, linear, isotropic media, no free surface current, and normal incidence, let \(\eta_i=\sqrt{\mu_i/\varepsilon_i}\) be the wave impedance. Electric fields are polarized in \(\hat{\mathbf x}\), with incident and transmitted waves traveling in \(+\hat{\mathbf z}\) and the reflected wave in \(-\hat{\mathbf z}\).

Continuity of tangential \(\mathbf E\) and \(\mathbf H\) gives

\[E_i+E_r=E_t,\qquad\frac{E_i-E_r}{\eta_1}=\frac{E_t}{\eta_2}.\]

Set \(r=E_r/E_i\), \(t=E_t/E_i\), substitute \(t=1+r\), and solve:

\[\frac{1-r}{\eta_1}=\frac{1+r}{\eta_2},\] \[\eta_2-\eta_2r=\eta_1+\eta_1r,\] \[\boxed{r=\frac{\eta_2-\eta_1}{\eta_2+\eta_1}},\qquad\boxed{t=\frac{2\eta_2}{\eta_1+\eta_2}}.\]

The reflection arises because the tangential fields must satisfy both continuity conditions simultaneously when impedances differ. See Electromagnetics for the energy-flux relation.

06 / WAVE REFRACTION

Maxwell boundary conditions versus Snell's law

Snell variables: refractive indices \(n_1,n_2\) are dimensionless; angles \(\theta\) are in radians (dimensionless in SI); \(k\) in \(\mathrm{rad/m}\), \(\omega\) in \(\mathrm{rad/s}\), and \(c\) in \(\mathrm{m/s}\).

Snell's law: wave direction, not a substitute for Maxwell's conditions

Statement. For monochromatic plane waves incident on a stationary flat interface between homogeneous isotropic positive-index media, phase matching yields \(n_1\sin\theta_i=n_2\sin\theta_t\). Reflection obeys \(\theta_r=\theta_i\).

Physical meaning. The wave's phase cannot slip discontinuously along a uniform interface. The tangential wavevector must therefore be shared by the incident, reflected and transmitted waves, while the magnitude \(k=n\omega/c\) changes between media.

Assumptions. Angles are measured from the normal; frequency is conserved at a stationary interface. The simple scalar Snell formula must be qualified for anisotropic, lossy or patterned media. It does not state that the complete electric-field vector is continuous.

They are related but are not the same. The four Maxwell interface conditions govern field components. They also apply to static fields. Snell's law governs the direction of refracted waves, and follows from applying field continuity and phase matching to a monochromatic wave at an ordinary uniform interface.

Maxwell's interface conditions

\[\boxed{\hat{\mathbf n}\cdot(\mathbf D_2-\mathbf D_1)=\sigma_f},\quad \boxed{\hat{\mathbf n}\cdot(\mathbf B_2-\mathbf B_1)=0}\] \[\boxed{\hat{\mathbf n}\times(\mathbf E_2-\mathbf E_1)=\mathbf0},\quad \boxed{\hat{\mathbf n}\times(\mathbf H_2-\mathbf H_1)=\mathbf K_f}\]

For zero free sheet charge and sheet current, \(D_n\), \(B_n\), \(E_t\), \(H_t\) are continuous. This does not imply all components of \(\mathbf E\) or \(\mathbf B\) are continuous when \(\varepsilon\) and \(\mu\) change.

Derive Snell's law from phase matching at the interface

Consider a stationary uniform planar interface at \(z=0\). Suppose incident, reflected and transmitted plane waves have the same angular frequency \(\omega\). For a wave,

\[\mathbf E(\mathbf r,t)=\operatorname{Re}\{\widetilde{\mathbf E}_0e^{i(\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r-\omega t)}\}.\]

For no free surface current, tangential \(\mathbf E\) and \(\mathbf H\) must be continuous at every point along the interface and at all times. The phases along the interface must therefore match:

\[\boxed{\mathbf k_{i,\parallel}=\mathbf k_{r,\parallel} =\mathbf k_{t,\parallel}}.\]

With angles \(\theta_i,\theta_r,\theta_t\) measured from the normal, tangential wavevector magnitude equals \(k\sin\theta\). Hence

\[k_1\sin\theta_i=k_1\sin\theta_r=k_2\sin\theta_t.\]

For homogeneous isotropic lossless positive-index media, \(k_j=n_j\omega/c\) where \(n_j=\sqrt{\varepsilon_j\mu_j/(\varepsilon_0\mu_0)}\). Cancel \(\omega/c\):

\[\boxed{\theta_r=\theta_i},\qquad \boxed{n_1\sin\theta_i=n_2\sin\theta_t}.\]

Physical meaning: Maxwell's conditions constrain the electric and magnetic fields; translational symmetry and phase matching determine reflection and refraction directions. The conditions together with wave impedances also determine reflected and transmitted amplitudes.

Worked example: vacuum into a nonmagnetic dielectric

Suppose \(\varepsilon_{r,2}=4\), \(\mu_{r,2}=1\). Vacuum has \(n_1=1\), while the dielectric has \(n_2=\sqrt4=2\). Then

\[\sin\theta_t=\frac12\sin\theta_i,\qquad \boxed{\theta_t=\arcsin\left(\frac{\sin\theta_i}{2}\right)}.\]

The transmitted wave bends toward the normal. However, it would be incorrect to infer that \(\mathbf E_1=\mathbf E_2\) as full vectors: only the tangential electric components are generally continuous.

Critical angle and limitations

For \(n_1>n_2\), the critical angle is \(\theta_c=\arcsin(n_2/n_1)\). Above it, the transmitted wave is evanescent rather than propagating; a lossless interface exhibits total internal reflection.

Complex refractive indices, anisotropy, patterned interfaces and moving boundaries can require a more general treatment than the scalar Snell equation. See normal-incidence field reflection/transmission.

06 / WORKED EXAMPLES

Electrostatic boundary-value problems

For every worked answer: potential \(V,V_0\) in \(\mathrm{V}\); \(\mathbf E\) in \(\mathrm{V/m}\); lengths in \(\mathrm{m}\); angles in radians. Each logarithm takes a dimensionless ratio of lengths.

Before solving: Laplace's equation and the uniqueness theorem

Statement. In a charge-free homogeneous region, the electrostatic potential satisfies \(\nabla^2 V=0\). If its value is specified on the entire boundary (a Dirichlet problem), there is at most one solution under the usual regularity conditions.

Physical meaning. The conductors fix voltages at surfaces; those constraints and the geometry determine the potential between them. Once \(V\) is found, the electric field follows from the negative gradient, including the appropriate cylindrical or spherical coordinate factors.

Assumptions and approach. First identify the charge-free region and its geometry. Use symmetry only where physically justified. Distinguish solving the Laplace boundary-value problem from deriving Maxwell's jump conditions with a pillbox or loop. For a region with nonzero charge density, solve Poisson's equation instead.

For Maxwell interface and wave applications, there is also a separate set of eight comprehensive-exam questions with worked solutions. These eight examples follow the accompanying MS Physics Boundary Conditions Worksheet With Solutions, Problems 1–8, in their original sequence. They concern Laplace boundary-value problems: conductor potentials at specified boundaries determine the electric field in a charge-free region. This is related to, but distinct from, the pillbox and loop jump conditions derived above.

Method for every problem

1. Specify the charge-free region and prescribed potentials. 2. Choose the appropriate coordinates and justify symmetry. 3. Reduce \(\nabla^2V=0\) to an ordinary differential equation. 4. Integrate and impose all boundary conditions. 5. Calculate \(\mathbf E=-\nabla V\) with the correct coordinate scale factors. 6. Check the limiting potentials, field direction, and any geometric idealizations.

Problem 1 · CartesianTwo parallel conducting walls

Problem

Two large parallel conducting walls lie at \(x=0\) and \(x=d\). The first is grounded; the second is held at \(V_0\). Between them there is no free charge, and edge effects are neglected. Find \(V(x)\) and \(\mathbf E\).

\[\boxed{V(0)=0,\qquad V(d)=V_0,\qquad 0<x<d.}\]

Principle before derivation: 1D Laplace equation

Statement. With no volume charge and planar symmetry, \(\nabla^2V=0\) becomes \(V''(x)=0\). Thus potential is linear, and its negative gradient is a uniform field.

Assumptions. Fixed wall voltages, large plates and negligible edge effects. Both integration constants come from the two boundary conditions.

1. Reduce Laplace's equation

Translational symmetry in \(y\) and \(z\) implies \(V=V(x)\).

\[\nabla^2V=\frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial x^2}+\frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial y^2}+\frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial z^2}=0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad\frac{d^2V}{dx^2}=0.\]

2. Integrate

\[\frac{dV}{dx}=A,\qquad V(x)=Ax+B.\]

3. Apply the two boundary conditions

\[V(0)=0\Rightarrow B=0,\qquad V(d)=V_0\Rightarrow Ad=V_0\Rightarrow A=\frac{V_0}{d}.\]

4. Differentiate to find the field

\[\mathbf E=-\nabla V=-\frac{dV}{dx}\hat{\mathbf x}.\]

Final answer

\[\boxed{V(x)=\frac{V_0x}{d},\qquad \mathbf E=-\frac{V_0}{d}\hat{\mathbf x}.}\]

Final answer units: Potential \([V(x)]=\mathrm{V}\); field \([\mathbf E]=\mathrm{V/m}\). The fraction \(x/d\) is dimensionless; \(V_0/d\) is in \(\mathrm{V/m}\).

Physical check: for \(V_0>0\), the field points from the higher-potential wall at \(x=d\) toward the grounded wall, and its magnitude is constant.

Problem 2 · CartesianThree parallel conducting sheets

Problem

Three infinite conducting sheets are at \(z=0\), \(z=h\), and \(z=2h\). The lower and upper sheets are grounded; the middle sheet is held at \(V_0\). Both gaps are charge-free and edge effects are neglected. Find the potential and electric field separately in each gap.

\[\boxed{V(0)=0,\qquad V(h)=V_0,\qquad V(2h)=0.}\]

Principle before derivation: solve each separated region

Statement. Every charge-free gap satisfies a separate 1D Laplace equation, so \(V(z)\) is piecewise linear. Conducting sheets fix potential at their positions.

Physical meaning. The middle high-voltage sheet creates oppositely directed fields in the two gaps. A field jump across a conducting sheet is compatible with surface charge.

1. Reduce Laplace's equation in each region

By plane symmetry, \(V=V(z)\). In each gap, \(d^2V_i/dz^2=0\), so \(V_i=A_i z+B_i\). A conductor at \(z=h\) separates the two intervals: each has its own constants.

2. Lower gap: \(0<z<h\)

\[V_1(z)=A_1z+B_1,\quad V_1(0)=0\Rightarrow B_1=0,\] \[V_1(h)=V_0\Rightarrow A_1=\frac{V_0}{h},\quad V_1(z)=\frac{V_0z}{h}.\] \[\mathbf E_1=-\frac{dV_1}{dz}\hat{\mathbf z}=-\frac{V_0}{h}\hat{\mathbf z}.\]

3. Upper gap: \(h<z<2h\)

\[V_2(z)=A_2z+B_2,\quad A_2h+B_2=V_0,\quad 2A_2h+B_2=0.\]

Subtract the two equations: \(A_2=-V_0/h\). Then \(B_2=2V_0\).

\[V_2(z)=V_0\left(2-\frac zh\right),\qquad \mathbf E_2=+\frac{V_0}{h}\hat{\mathbf z}.\]

Final answer

\[V(z)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{V_0z}{h},&0\le z\le h,\\[4pt] V_0\left(2-\dfrac zh\right),&h\le z\le 2h,\end{cases}\] \[\mathbf E(z)=\begin{cases}-\dfrac{V_0}{h}\hat{\mathbf z},&0<z<h,\\[4pt] +\dfrac{V_0}{h}\hat{\mathbf z},&h<z<2h.\end{cases}\]

Final answer units: Potentials \([V_1]=[V_2]=\mathrm{V}\); fields \([\mathbf E_1]=[\mathbf E_2]=\mathrm{V/m}\). \(V_0/h\) is a voltage divided by a length.

Physical check: for \(V_0>0\), the electric field points away from the middle conductor into both gaps. Its normal component may jump across a charged conducting sheet.

Problem 3 · Rotated CartesianTwo inclined parallel conducting plates

Problem

Two infinite parallel conducting plates lie on \(x\cos\alpha+y\sin\alpha=0\) and \(x\cos\alpha+y\sin\alpha=d\). The first is grounded; the other is at \(V_0\). The gap is charge-free. Use the normal coordinate \(u=x\cos\alpha+y\sin\alpha\) to find \(V(x,y)\) and \(\mathbf E\).

Principle before derivation: rotate into the normal direction

Statement. If the conductor surfaces depend only on \(u=x\cos\alpha+y\sin\alpha\), then symmetry permits \(V=V(u)\), reducing Laplace's equation to \(d^2V/du^2=0\).

Physical meaning. The field is normal to the parallel conducting surfaces and does not depend separately on the original Cartesian coordinates.

1. Express derivatives in the normal coordinate

Symmetry implies \(V=V(u)\). The chain rule gives

\[\frac{\partial V}{\partial x}=\frac{dV}{du}\cos\alpha,\qquad \frac{\partial V}{\partial y}=\frac{dV}{du}\sin\alpha.\] \[\frac{\partial^2V}{\partial x^2}=\frac{d^2V}{du^2}\cos^2\alpha,\qquad \frac{\partial^2V}{\partial y^2}=\frac{d^2V}{du^2}\sin^2\alpha.\]

2. Reduce and integrate Laplace's equation

\[\nabla^2V=\left(\cos^2\alpha+\sin^2\alpha\right)\frac{d^2V}{du^2} =\frac{d^2V}{du^2}=0.\] \[V(u)=Au+B.\]

3. Apply \(V(0)=0\), \(V(d)=V_0\)

\[B=0,\quad A=\frac{V_0}{d},\quad V(x,y)=\frac{V_0}{d}(x\cos\alpha+y\sin\alpha).\]

4. Evaluate the gradient

\[\nabla V=\frac{V_0}{d}\left(\cos\alpha\,\hat{\mathbf x}+\sin\alpha\,\hat{\mathbf y}\right).\]

Final answer

\[\boxed{V(x,y)=\frac{V_0}{d}(x\cos\alpha+y\sin\alpha)}\] \[\boxed{\mathbf E=-\frac{V_0}{d}\left(\cos\alpha\,\hat{\mathbf x}+\sin\alpha\,\hat{\mathbf y}\right).}\]

Final answer units: Potential \([V]=\mathrm{V}\); field \([\mathbf E]=\mathrm{V/m}\). \(x,y,d\) are in \(\mathrm{m}\), while \(\alpha\), \(\sin\alpha\) and \(\cos\alpha\) are dimensionless.

Physical check: the field is perpendicular to both plates; \(|\mathbf E|=|V_0|/d\). Rotating the plates changes field direction, not magnitude.

Problem 4 · CylindricalTwo concentric conducting cylinders

Problem

Two long coaxial conducting cylinders have radii \(0<a<b\). The inner cylinder is held at \(V_0\); the outer is grounded. The annular region \(a<r<b\) is charge-free; neglect end effects. Find \(V(r)\) and \(\mathbf E(r)\).

\[\boxed{V(a)=V_0,\qquad V(b)=0.}\]

Principle before derivation: cylindrical radial Laplace equation

Statement. With cylindrical symmetry and no volume charge between long coaxial cylinders, \((1/r)d[\,rV'(r)\,]/dr=0\), so the potential is logarithmic in radius.

Physical meaning. The radial field scales as \(1/r\), since the same electric flux spreads over larger cylindrical surfaces.

1. Reduce the cylindrical Laplacian

Cylindrical symmetry implies \(V=V(r)\):

\[\nabla^2V=\frac1r\frac{d}{dr}\left(r\frac{dV}{dr}\right)=0.\]

2. Integrate the radial equation

\[\frac{d}{dr}\left(r\frac{dV}{dr}\right)=0 \Rightarrow r\frac{dV}{dr}=A\Rightarrow \frac{dV}{dr}=\frac Ar.\]

Integrating again with a dimensionless logarithm,

\[V(r)=A\ln\left(\frac ra\right)+B.\]

3. Enforce both potentials

\[V(a)=V_0\Rightarrow B=V_0,\qquad V(b)=0\Rightarrow A\ln(b/a)+V_0=0.\] \[A=-\frac{V_0}{\ln(b/a)}.\]

4. Find the radial electric field

\[\mathbf E=-\frac{dV}{dr}\hat{\mathbf r}=-\frac Ar\hat{\mathbf r}.\]

Final answer

\[\boxed{V(r)=V_0\frac{\ln(b/r)}{\ln(b/a)}}\] \[\boxed{\mathbf E(r)=\frac{V_0}{r\ln(b/a)}\hat{\mathbf r}.}\]

Final answer units: Potential \([V(r)]=\mathrm{V}\); field \([\mathbf E]=\mathrm{V/m}\). The arguments \(b/r\) and \(b/a\) of the logarithms are dimensionless.

Physical check: if \(V_0>0\), the field points radially outward and falls as \(1/r\), unlike the \(1/r^2\) field between concentric spheres.

Problem 5 · CylindricalConducting radial walls forming a wedge

Problem

Two infinite conducting half-planes meet along the \(z\)-axis at \(\phi_1=\pi/6\) (grounded) and \(\phi_2=2\pi/3\) (at \(V_0\)). The interior is charge-free. Under the idealized assumption \(V=V(\phi)\), find \(V(\phi)\) and \(\mathbf E(r,\phi)\).

\[\boxed{V(\pi/6)=0,\qquad V(2\pi/3)=V_0.}\]

Principle before derivation: angular Laplace equation

Statement. If the potential between radial conducting walls depends only on the cylindrical azimuthal angle \(\phi\), the charge-free Laplace equation reduces to \(V''(\phi)=0\).

Physical meaning. The potential is linear in angle, but the physical field contains the coordinate factor \(1/r\); angular derivatives are not lengths.

1. Reduce cylindrical Laplace's equation

\[\nabla^2V=\frac1r\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\left(r\frac{\partial V}{\partial r}\right)+\frac1{r^2}\frac{\partial^2V}{\partial\phi^2}+\frac{\partial^2V}{\partial z^2}=0.\]

Since \(V=V(\phi)\), only the angular term survives:

\[\frac1{r^2}\frac{d^2V}{d\phi^2}=0\Rightarrow\frac{d^2V}{d\phi^2}=0.\]

2. Integrate and impose boundaries

\[V(\phi)=A\phi+B,\qquad V(\pi/6)=0\Rightarrow B=-A\frac\pi6.\] \[V(2\pi/3)=A\left(\frac{2\pi}3-\frac\pi6\right)=A\frac\pi2=V_0.\] \[A=\frac{2V_0}{\pi}.\]

3. Use the cylindrical gradient

\[\nabla V=\hat{\mathbf r}\,\partial_rV+\frac{\hat{\boldsymbol\phi}}{r}\,\partial_\phi V+\hat{\mathbf z}\,\partial_zV.\] \[\mathbf E=-\frac1r\frac{dV}{d\phi}\hat{\boldsymbol\phi}.\]

Final answer

\[\boxed{V(\phi)=\frac{2V_0}{\pi}\left(\phi-\frac\pi6\right)}\] \[\boxed{\mathbf E(r,\phi)=-\frac{2V_0}{\pi r}\hat{\boldsymbol\phi}.}\]

Final answer units: Potential \([V(\phi)]=\mathrm{V}\); field \([\mathbf E]=\mathrm{V/m}\). Angles are in radians (dimensionless), and \(1/r\) contributes \(\mathrm{m^{-1}}\).

Physical check: an angular increment \(d\phi\) corresponds to an arc length \(r\,d\phi\), explaining the factor \(1/r\) in the field even though the potential depends only on \(\phi\). The idealized field diverges near the sharp meeting edge \(r=0\).

Problem 6 · CylindricalThree concentric cylindrical electrodes

Problem

Three long coaxial conducting cylinders have radii \(0<a<b<c\). The inner and outer cylinders are grounded; the middle cylinder is held at \(V_0\). Both annular gaps are charge-free, with end effects neglected. Find \(V(r)\) and \(\mathbf E(r)\) in each gap.

\[\boxed{V(a)=0,\qquad V(b)=V_0,\qquad V(c)=0.}\]

Principle before derivation: separate cylindrical gaps

Statement. Each region between adjacent concentric electrodes has its own logarithmic solution and boundary constants. A conductor fixes the potential at its radius.

Physical meaning. The electric field can reverse direction across the middle electrode because that conductor is separately charged on its two faces.

1. Reduce and solve in each radial interval

\[\frac1r\frac{d}{dr}\left(r\frac{dV_i}{dr}\right)=0\Rightarrow V_i(r)=A_i\ln r+B_i.\]

The middle conductor separates two regions; determine their constants independently.

2. Inner gap: \(a<r<b\)

\[V_1=A_1\ln(r/a)+B_1,\qquad V_1(a)=0\Rightarrow B_1=0.\] \[V_1(b)=V_0\Rightarrow A_1=\frac{V_0}{\ln(b/a)}.\] \[V_1(r)=V_0\frac{\ln(r/a)}{\ln(b/a)},\qquad \mathbf E_1=-\frac{V_0}{r\ln(b/a)}\hat{\mathbf r}.\]

3. Outer gap: \(b<r<c\)

\[V_2=A_2\ln(r/c)+B_2,\qquad V_2(c)=0\Rightarrow B_2=0.\] \[V_2(b)=V_0\Rightarrow A_2=\frac{V_0}{\ln(b/c)}=-\frac{V_0}{\ln(c/b)}.\] \[V_2(r)=V_0\frac{\ln(c/r)}{\ln(c/b)},\qquad \mathbf E_2=+\frac{V_0}{r\ln(c/b)}\hat{\mathbf r}.\]

Final answer

\[V(r)=\begin{cases} \dfrac{V_0\ln(r/a)}{\ln(b/a)},&a<r<b,\\[5pt] \dfrac{V_0\ln(c/r)}{\ln(c/b)},&b<r<c, \end{cases}\] \[\mathbf E(r)=\begin{cases} -\dfrac{V_0}{r\ln(b/a)}\hat{\mathbf r},&a<r<b,\\[5pt] +\dfrac{V_0}{r\ln(c/b)}\hat{\mathbf r},&b<r<c. \end{cases}\]

Final answer units: Potentials \([V_1]=[V_2]=\mathrm{V}\); fields \([\mathbf E_1]=[\mathbf E_2]=\mathrm{V/m}\). All logarithm arguments are dimensionless length ratios.

Physical check: for \(V_0>0\), the field points away from the middle conductor, inward in the inner gap and outward in the outer gap.

Problem 7 · SphericalTwo concentric conducting spheres

Problem

Two concentric conducting shells have radii \(0<a<b\). The inner shell is at \(V_0\); the outer shell is grounded. The gap \(a<r<b\) contains no free charge. Assume spherical symmetry. Derive \(V(r)\) and \(\mathbf E(r)\), and compare their radial behavior with concentric cylinders.

\[\boxed{V(a)=V_0,\qquad V(b)=0.}\]

Principle before derivation: spherical radial Laplace equation

Statement. In a spherically symmetric charge-free region, \((1/r^2)d[\,r^2V'(r)\,]/dr=0\). The solution is a constant plus a term proportional to \(1/r\).

Physical meaning. The radial field scales as \(1/r^2\), consistent with flux spreading over a sphere of area \(4\pi r^2\).

1. Reduce spherical Laplace's equation

\[\nabla^2V=\frac1{r^2}\frac{d}{dr}\left(r^2\frac{dV}{dr}\right)=0.\]

2. Integrate twice

\[\frac{d}{dr}\left(r^2\frac{dV}{dr}\right)=0\Rightarrow r^2\frac{dV}{dr}=A\Rightarrow\frac{dV}{dr}=\frac{A}{r^2}.\] \[V(r)=-\frac Ar+B=\frac Cr+D.\]

3. Apply the two potentials

\[\frac Ca+D=V_0,\qquad\frac Cb+D=0.\]

Subtracting,

\[C\left(\frac1a-\frac1b\right)=V_0\Rightarrow C=\frac{V_0}{1/a-1/b}=\frac{V_0ab}{b-a}.\] \[D=-\frac Cb,\qquad V(r)=C\left(\frac1r-\frac1b\right).\]

4. Calculate the radial field

\[\mathbf E=-\frac{dV}{dr}\hat{\mathbf r} =\frac{C}{r^2}\hat{\mathbf r}.\]

Final answer

\[\boxed{V(r)=V_0\frac{1/r-1/b}{1/a-1/b}}\] \[\boxed{\mathbf E(r)=\frac{V_0ab}{(b-a)r^2}\hat{\mathbf r}.}\]

Final answer units: Potential \([V]=\mathrm{V}\); field \([\mathbf E]=\mathrm{V/m}\). \(ab/[(b-a)r^2]\) has units \(\mathrm{m^{-1}}\).

Physical check: the spherical field decays as \(1/r^2\), whereas the field between infinitely long concentric cylinders decays as \(1/r\). The difference follows from the radial scale factors in the respective Laplacians.

Problem 8 · SphericalConducting cone above a grounded plane

Problem

An ideal conducting cone has its apex at the origin, its axis along \(+\hat{\mathbf z}\), and its surface at polar angle \(\theta=\theta_0\), where \(0<\theta_0<\pi/2\). The cone is held at \(V_0\); an infinite grounded plane occupies \(\theta=\pi/2\). The wedge-like region between them is charge-free. Assume \(V=V(\theta)\). Find \(V(\theta)\), \(\mathbf E(r,\theta)\), and its direction for \(V_0>0\).

\[\boxed{V(\theta_0)=V_0,\qquad V(\pi/2)=0.}\]

Principle before derivation: polar-angle Laplace equation

Statement. When a spherical electrostatic boundary problem depends only on polar angle \(\theta\), the angular Laplacian becomes \((1/\sin\theta)d[\sin\theta\,V'(\theta)]/d\theta=0\).

Physical meaning. The potential can depend on angle even with no free volume charge; the resulting electric field must include the spherical gradient factor \(1/r\). Avoid singular boundary directions outside the stated angular domain.

1. Reduce spherical Laplace's equation

With \(V=V(\theta)\), the spherical Laplacian reduces to

\[\nabla^2V=\frac1{r^2\sin\theta}\frac{d}{d\theta}\left(\sin\theta\frac{dV}{d\theta}\right)=0.\]

Multiply by \(r^2\sin\theta\) in the interior region:

\[\frac{d}{d\theta}\left(\sin\theta\frac{dV}{d\theta}\right)=0.\]

2. Integrate the angular equation

\[\sin\theta\frac{dV}{d\theta}=A\Rightarrow \frac{dV}{d\theta}=A\csc\theta.\]

Use the identity \(\int \csc\theta\,d\theta=\ln\tan(\theta/2)\) for \(0<\theta<\pi\):

\[V(\theta)=A\ln\tan\left(\frac\theta2\right)+B.\]

3. Apply both boundary conditions

\[V(\pi/2)=A\ln\tan(\pi/4)+B=B=0.\] \[V(\theta_0)=A\ln\tan(\theta_0/2)=V_0\Rightarrow A=\frac{V_0}{\ln\tan(\theta_0/2)}.\]

4. Differentiate using the spherical gradient

\[\mathbf E=-\nabla V=-\frac1r\frac{dV}{d\theta}\hat{\boldsymbol\theta} =-\frac{A}{r\sin\theta}\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}.\]

Final answer

\[\boxed{V(\theta)=V_0\frac{\ln\tan(\theta/2)}{\ln\tan(\theta_0/2)}}\] \[\boxed{\mathbf E(r,\theta)=-\frac{V_0}{r\sin\theta\,\ln\tan(\theta_0/2)}\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}.}\]

Final answer units: Potential \([V]=\mathrm{V}\); field \([\mathbf E]=\mathrm{V/m}\). Angular and logarithmic factors are dimensionless; \(1/r\) contributes \(\mathrm{m^{-1}}\).

Physical check: for \(0<\theta_0<\pi/2\), \(\ln\tan(\theta_0/2)<0\); therefore when \(V_0>0\), the electric field points in \(+\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}\), from the cone toward the grounded plane. The idealization produces a \(1/r\) singularity near the sharp apex.

Quick reference · coordinate forms and checks (worksheet p. 9)

For a charge-free region,

\[\boxed{\nabla^2V=0},\qquad\boxed{\mathbf E=-\nabla V}.\]

Use the following symmetry-reduced equations and general solutions before applying boundary potentials:

Cartesian

\[\frac{d^2V}{dx^2}=0\quad\Rightarrow\quad V=Ax+B.\]

Cylindrical · radial

\[\frac1r\frac d{dr}\left(r\frac{dV}{dr}\right)=0 \Rightarrow V=A\ln(r/r_*)+B.\]

Cylindrical · azimuthal

\[\frac{d^2V}{d\phi^2}=0\Rightarrow V=A\phi+B.\]

Spherical · radial

\[\frac1{r^2}\frac d{dr}\left(r^2\frac{dV}{dr}\right)=0 \Rightarrow V=\frac Ar+B.\]

Spherical · polar

\[\frac1{\sin\theta}\frac d{d\theta}\left(\sin\theta\frac{dV}{d\theta}\right)=0 \Rightarrow V=A\ln\tan(\theta/2)+B.\]

The reference length \(r_*>0\) makes the logarithm argument dimensionless.

Gradient scale factors

\[\nabla V_{\rm cyl}=\hat{\mathbf r}\partial_rV+ \frac{\hat{\boldsymbol\phi}}{r}\partial_\phi V+\hat{\mathbf z}\partial_z V.\] \[\nabla V_{\rm sph}=\hat{\mathbf r}\partial_rV+ \frac{\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}}r\partial_\theta V+ \frac{\hat{\boldsymbol\phi}}{r\sin\theta}\partial_\phi V.\]

Exam check: state the domain and potentials; justify symmetry; solve the correct ODE; impose every boundary value; differentiate with the metric factors; verify potential limits and field direction. Finite electrodes and sharp edges may require more general solutions.

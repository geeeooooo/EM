Conventions: SI units; \(\partial\mathcal V\) denotes a closed surface enclosing volume \(\mathcal V\); \(\partial\mathcal S\) denotes the boundary curve of an oriented surface \(\mathcal S\); \(d\mathbf a=\hat{\mathbf n}\,dA\). Surface/contour orientations obey the right-hand rule. Unless stated otherwise, surfaces and contours are fixed in space.

SI units and physical constants

Fields: \(\mathbf E\): electric field, \(\mathrm{V/m}=\mathrm{N/C}\); \(\mathbf D\): displacement, \(\mathrm{C/m^2}\); \(\mathbf B\): flux density, tesla \(\mathrm{T}=\mathrm{Wb/m^2}\); \(\mathbf H\): field intensity, \(\mathrm{A/m}\); \(V\): electric potential, volts \(\mathrm{V}\).

Sources: \(Q\): charge, coulombs \(\mathrm{C}\); \(\rho,\rho_f\): volume charge density, \(\mathrm{C/m^3}\); \(\sigma_f\): surface charge density, \(\mathrm{C/m^2}\); \(\mathbf J,\mathbf J_f\): current density, \(\mathrm{A/m^2}\); \(\mathbf K_f\): surface current density, \(\mathrm{A/m}\).

Materials: \(\varepsilon,\varepsilon_0\): permittivity, \(\mathrm{F/m}\); \(\mu,\mu_0\): permeability, \(\mathrm{H/m}\); \(\mathbf P\): polarization, \(\mathrm{C/m^2}\); \(\mathbf M\): magnetization, \(\mathrm{A/m}\); \(\sigma\): conductivity, \(\mathrm{S/m}\); \(\eta\): wave impedance, ohms \(\Omega\). Relative permittivity, relative permeability and refractive index \(n\) are dimensionless.

Vacuum constants:

\[\varepsilon_0\approx8.854\times10^{-12}\ \mathrm{F/m},\quad \mu_0\approx1.257\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{H/m}\] \[c=299\,792\,458\ \mathrm{m/s},\quad \eta_0\approx377\ \Omega\]

The vacuum speed of light \(c\) is exact by SI definition; the other displayed values are rounded.

Space and waves: lengths \(\mathrm{m}\), area \(\mathrm{m^2}\), time \(\mathrm{s}\); \(\omega\): angular frequency, \(\mathrm{rad/s}\); \(k\): wavenumber, \(\mathrm{rad/m}\); \(v\): propagation speed, \(\mathrm{m/s}\). Angles in radians are dimensionless in SI. Logarithms and exponentials must have dimensionless arguments.

Energy: energy density \(u\): \(\mathrm{J/m^3}\); Poynting vector \(\mathbf S\): \(\mathrm{W/m^2}\); power delivered to charges per volume \(\mathbf J\cdot\mathbf E\): \(\mathrm{W/m^3}\). Magnetic flux \(\Phi_B\): webers \(\mathrm{Wb}\); displacement flux \(\Phi_D\): coulombs \(\mathrm{C}\).

01 / MATHEMATICAL TOOLKIT

Why differential and integral forms are equivalent

Unit check: each spatial derivative contributes inverse metres \(\mathrm{m^{-1}}\); a surface integral introduces \(\mathrm{m^2}\) and a line integral introduces \(\mathrm{m}\).

Before the proofs: what the integral theorems say

Statement. The divergence theorem converts the total divergence inside a volume into flux through its closed boundary; Stokes' theorem converts the normal component of curl through an open surface into circulation around its oriented boundary.

Physical meaning. Divergence measures how strongly field lines originate or terminate locally; curl measures local circulation. Summing local contributions over a region produces the global flux or loop integral.

Assumptions and use. Apply the theorems to sufficiently smooth fields on properly oriented regions, or use generalized distributional forms for singular sources. Stokes' right-hand orientation links the surface normal to the path direction. The equivalence of integral and differential Maxwell equations follows by applying these identities to arbitrary admissible regions.

Differential form states a local relation at every point; integral form states the corresponding total flux or circulation over a finite region. Two theorems bridge the descriptions.

Reading the operators physically

Divergence: \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf F\) measures net outward flux per unit volume in the infinitesimal limit. It does not measure the size of the field alone. A nonzero field can have zero divergence.

Curl: \(\nabla\times\mathbf F\) measures local circulation per unit area in the infinitesimal limit; its direction specifies the circulation axis by the right-hand rule. Curl is not the same thing as the direction or strength of the field.

Flux: \(\int_{\mathcal S}\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf a\) measures the component crossing a surface. Circulation: \(\oint_{\partial\mathcal S}\mathbf F\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell\) measures the component tangent to a closed path. This is why Gauss laws use closed surfaces, whereas Faraday and Ampère–Maxwell laws use loops.

Divergence theorem

\[\boxed{\oint_{\partial\mathcal V}\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf a=\int_{\mathcal V}(\nabla\cdot\mathbf F)\,dV}\]

Converts flux through a closed surface into divergence throughout the enclosed volume.

Stokes' theorem

\[\boxed{\oint_{\partial\mathcal S}\mathbf F\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell=\int_{\mathcal S}(\nabla\times\mathbf F)\cdot d\mathbf a}\]

Converts circulation around a loop into curl through its spanning surface.

General proof · Divergence ↔ flux

Suppose \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf F=s\). Integrate over an arbitrary fixed volume:

\[\int_{\mathcal V}\nabla\cdot\mathbf F\,dV=\int_{\mathcal V}s\,dV.\]

Apply the divergence theorem to the left:

\[\boxed{\oint_{\partial\mathcal V}\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf a=\int_{\mathcal V}s\,dV}.\]

Conversely, begin with the boxed statement for every suitable volume. Apply the theorem backward:

\[\int_{\mathcal V}(\nabla\cdot\mathbf F-s)\,dV=0.\]

Because this holds for arbitrarily small volumes around every point (assuming sufficiently regular fields),

\[\boxed{\nabla\cdot\mathbf F=s}.\]

One particular finite volume would not suffice to infer a pointwise equation: the equality must hold for every volume.

General proof · Curl ↔ circulation

Suppose \(\nabla\times\mathbf F=\mathbf g\). Integrate the normal component over an arbitrary oriented surface:

\[\int_{\mathcal S}(\nabla\times\mathbf F)\cdot d\mathbf a=\int_{\mathcal S}\mathbf g\cdot d\mathbf a.\]

By Stokes' theorem, the left becomes a contour integral:

\[\boxed{\oint_{\partial\mathcal S}\mathbf F\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell=\int_{\mathcal S}\mathbf g\cdot d\mathbf a}.\]

Conversely, apply Stokes to the boxed formula for all suitably oriented surfaces:

\[\int_{\mathcal S}(\nabla\times\mathbf F-\mathbf g)\cdot d\mathbf a=0.\]

Taking arbitrarily small surface patches in any normal direction yields

\[\boxed{\nabla\times\mathbf F=\mathbf g}.\]

The loop direction and surface normal are linked by the right-hand rule.

02 / VACUUM

Maxwell's equations in vacuum

Units introduced: \(\rho\): \(\mathrm{C/m^3}\); \(\mathbf J\): \(\mathrm{A/m^2}\); \(\mathbf E\): \(\mathrm{V/m}\); \(\mathbf B\): tesla \(\mathrm{T}\); \(\varepsilon_0\): \(\mathrm{F/m}\); \(\mu_0\): \(\mathrm{H/m}\).

Understand the four Maxwell laws before deriving them

Electric Gauss law. Electric charge creates electric flux through a closed surface; in differential form, charge density is the local source of electric-field divergence. A Gaussian surface is a mathematical integration boundary, not a physical conductor.

Magnetic Gauss law. The net magnetic flux through every closed surface is zero: magnetic field lines have no endpoints in ordinary Maxwell theory. Enclosing only one end of a bar magnet does not produce a net flux because field enters or exits elsewhere on the closed surface.

Faraday law. Changing magnetic flux creates circulation of the electric field. Ampère–Maxwell law. Both electric current and changing electric flux create magnetic circulation. The added displacement-current term is required for consistency with charge conservation, even where no conduction current crosses the surface.

Conditions. These local and integral laws apply generally, including time-dependent fields. The vacuum constitutive constants relate \(\mathbf E,\mathbf B\) to sources; simple electrostatic and magnetostatic reductions additionally require time independence.

Here \(\rho\) and \(\mathbf J\) are the total charge and current densities. “In vacuum” describes the vacuum field equations; sources such as isolated charged particles or wires may still be present.

I. Gauss's law · Electric

Differential\[\boxed{\nabla\cdot\mathbf E=\frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0}}\]Integral\[\boxed{\oint_{\partial\mathcal V}\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf a=\frac{Q_{\mathrm{enc}}}{\varepsilon_0}}\]

Electric charge produces electric flux.

Physical meaning

Local meaning: \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf E\) measures whether electric field has a net outward flux per unit volume around a point. Positive charge is a local source of electric flux; negative charge is a sink.

Global meaning: the net electric flux through a closed surface depends only on the total charge enclosed—not the surface shape or charges outside. Zero enclosed charge means zero net flux, not necessarily zero \(\mathbf E\) at the surface.

Derive both directions · Electric Gauss

Differential → Integral

\[\nabla\cdot\mathbf E=\frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0} \ \Rightarrow\ \int_{\mathcal V}\nabla\cdot\mathbf E\,dV =\frac{1}{\varepsilon_0}\int_{\mathcal V}\rho\,dV.\]

Use the divergence theorem and \(Q_{\mathrm{enc}}=\int_{\mathcal V}\rho\,dV\):

\[\oint_{\partial\mathcal V}\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf a=\frac{Q_{\mathrm{enc}}}{\varepsilon_0}.\]

Integral → Differential

\[\oint_{\partial\mathcal V}\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf a =\frac{1}{\varepsilon_0}\int_{\mathcal V}\rho\,dV\] \[\Rightarrow\ \int_{\mathcal V}\left(\nabla\cdot\mathbf E-\frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0}\right)dV=0.\]

For every volume \(\mathcal V\), the integrand must vanish, so \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf E=\rho/\varepsilon_0\).

II. Gauss's law · Magnetic

Differential\[\boxed{\nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0}\]Integral\[\boxed{\oint_{\partial\mathcal V}\mathbf B\cdot d\mathbf a=0}\]

No isolated magnetic charge appears in classical Maxwell theory: the net flux through any closed surface is zero.

Physical meaning

Local meaning: \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0\) says magnetic field has no local sources or sinks (no magnetic monopoles in classical Maxwell theory).

Global meaning: as much magnetic flux enters any closed surface as leaves it. A surface surrounding only the north end of a bar magnet still has compensating inward flux through its other portions, including surfaces cutting through the magnet. Zero net flux does not mean zero magnetic field.

Derive both directions · Magnetic Gauss

Differential → Integral

\[\int_{\mathcal V}\nabla\cdot\mathbf B\,dV=\int_{\mathcal V}0\,dV=0\] \[\Rightarrow\ \oint_{\partial\mathcal V}\mathbf B\cdot d\mathbf a=0.\]

Integral → Differential

\[\oint_{\partial\mathcal V}\mathbf B\cdot d\mathbf a=0 \Rightarrow\int_{\mathcal V}\nabla\cdot\mathbf B\,dV=0.\]

For arbitrary volumes, \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0\) pointwise. Field lines may exit and reenter a surface; the net flux cancels.

III. Faraday's law

Differential\[\boxed{\nabla\times\mathbf E=-\frac{\partial\mathbf B}{\partial t}}\]Integral, fixed loop\[\boxed{\oint_{\partial\mathcal S}\mathbf E\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell=-\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\mathcal S}\mathbf B\cdot d\mathbf a}\]

A changing magnetic field creates circulation of the electric field.

Physical meaning

Local meaning: a magnetic field changing with time produces a circulating, generally nonconservative electric field. The curl measures that local tendency to circulate—not the magnitude of \(\mathbf E\) itself.

Global meaning: changing magnetic flux through a stationary loop produces electric-field circulation around its boundary. The minus sign expresses Lenz's law: the induced circulation is oriented to oppose the flux change. A wire is not required for the induced electric field to exist.

Derive both directions · Faraday

Differential → Integral

\[\int_{\mathcal S}(\nabla\times\mathbf E)\cdot d\mathbf a=-\int_{\mathcal S}\frac{\partial\mathbf B}{\partial t}\cdot d\mathbf a.\]

Use Stokes' theorem on the left. For a fixed surface, interchange the time derivative and surface integral on the right:

\[\oint_{\partial\mathcal S}\mathbf E\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell=-\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\mathcal S}\mathbf B\cdot d\mathbf a.\]

Integral → Differential

\[\oint_{\partial\mathcal S}\mathbf E\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell+\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\mathcal S}\mathbf B\cdot d\mathbf a=0\] \[\Rightarrow\ \int_{\mathcal S}\left(\nabla\times\mathbf E+\frac{\partial\mathbf B}{\partial t}\right)\cdot d\mathbf a=0.\]

Arbitrary small oriented patches imply \(\nabla\times\mathbf E=-\partial\mathbf B/\partial t\).

For a moving conducting loop, the magnetic-flux rule for electromotive force also includes the magnetic force on moving charges; do not apply this fixed-loop formula unchanged.

IV. Ampère–Maxwell law

Differential\[\boxed{\nabla\times\mathbf B=\mu_0\mathbf J+\mu_0\varepsilon_0\frac{\partial\mathbf E}{\partial t}}\]Integral, fixed loop\[\boxed{\oint_{\partial\mathcal S}\mathbf B\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell=\mu_0I_{\mathrm{enc}}+\mu_0\varepsilon_0\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\mathcal S}\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf a}\]

Conduction and other currents, and a changing electric flux, produce magnetic circulation.

Physical meaning

Local meaning: electric current density and a time-varying electric field create magnetic-field curl. The direction of circulation follows the right-hand rule for the chosen surface orientation.

Global meaning: the circulation of \(\mathbf B\) around a loop is determined by current crossing a spanning surface plus the rate of change of electric flux through it. The displacement-current term allows consistent results for a charging capacitor, even across the insulating gap where no conduction current flows. Curl does not mean that \(\mathbf B\) points in the same direction as the current.

Derive both directions · Ampère–Maxwell

Differential → Integral

\[\int_{\mathcal S}(\nabla\times\mathbf B)\cdot d\mathbf a =\mu_0\int_{\mathcal S}\mathbf J\cdot d\mathbf a +\mu_0\varepsilon_0\int_{\mathcal S}\frac{\partial\mathbf E}{\partial t}\cdot d\mathbf a.\]

Apply Stokes and define \(I_{\mathrm{enc}}=\int_{\mathcal S}\mathbf J\cdot d\mathbf a\):

\[\oint_{\partial\mathcal S}\mathbf B\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell =\mu_0I_{\mathrm{enc}}+\mu_0\varepsilon_0\frac{d\Phi_E}{dt},\quad \Phi_E=\int_{\mathcal S}\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf a.\]

Integral → Differential

Write \(I_{\mathrm{enc}}\) as a surface integral; apply Stokes to the contour, then combine terms:

\[\int_{\mathcal S}\left(\nabla\times\mathbf B-\mu_0\mathbf J-\mu_0\varepsilon_0\frac{\partial\mathbf E}{\partial t}\right)\cdot d\mathbf a=0.\]

Because this holds for all small oriented patches,

\[\nabla\times\mathbf B=\mu_0\mathbf J+\mu_0\varepsilon_0\frac{\partial\mathbf E}{\partial t}.\]
Why the displacement-current term is necessary

Take the divergence of Ampère–Maxwell:

\[0=\nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf B)=\mu_0\nabla\cdot\mathbf J+\mu_0\varepsilon_0\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(\nabla\cdot\mathbf E).\]

By Gauss's law, \(\varepsilon_0\nabla\cdot\mathbf E=\rho\), so

\[\boxed{\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot\mathbf J=0}.\]

This is local charge conservation. Omitting the displacement term would incorrectly force \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf J=0\) even in situations where charge accumulates, such as a charging capacitor.

03 / MATERIALS

Maxwell's equations in matter

Units introduced: \(\mathbf D\): \(\mathrm{C/m^2}\); \(\mathbf H\): \(\mathrm{A/m}\); \(\rho_f\): \(\mathrm{C/m^3}\); \(\mathbf J_f\): \(\mathrm{A/m^2}\). These make the two sides of each macroscopic Maxwell equation dimensionally consistent.

Why the macroscopic field equations use D and H

Statement. In matter, microscopic charge and current are partitioned into free and bound sources. The auxiliary fields \(\mathbf D\) and \(\mathbf H\) incorporate polarization and magnetization so that Gauss's law for \(\mathbf D\) counts free charge and Ampère–Maxwell's law for \(\mathbf H\) counts free current.

Physical meaning. Dielectrics polarize and magnetic materials magnetize in response to fields. These material responses do not require a new type of Maxwell equation: they change how the total microscopic sources are represented macroscopically.

Assumptions and caution. The definitions \(\mathbf D=\varepsilon_0\mathbf E+\mathbf P\) and \(\mathbf H=\mathbf B/\mu_0-\mathbf M\) are general macroscopic identities, but \(\mathbf D=\varepsilon\mathbf E\) and \(\mathbf B=\mu\mathbf H\) additionally assume an appropriate linear, isotropic constitutive response. Never interpret \(\mathbf D\) or \(\mathbf H\) as an additional physical source independent of \(\mathbf E,\mathbf B\).

Macroscopic electromagnetism separates free sources from charges and currents bound within polarized/magnetized matter. The fields \(\mathbf E\) and \(\mathbf B\) remain physical; \(\mathbf D\) and \(\mathbf H\) package material response.

I. Electric Gauss, in matter

Differential\[\boxed{\nabla\cdot\mathbf D=\rho_f}\]Integral\[\boxed{\oint_{\partial\mathcal V}\mathbf D\cdot d\mathbf a=Q_{f,\mathrm{enc}}}\]

Physical meaning

Local meaning: only free charge is an explicit source of \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf D\). Polarization \(\mathbf P\) incorporates the effects of bound charges through \(\mathbf D=\varepsilon_0\mathbf E+\mathbf P\).

Global meaning: net \(\mathbf D\)-flux through a closed surface equals enclosed free charge. Bound charge still influences the actual electric field \(\mathbf E\); \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf D=0\) does not imply \(\mathbf D=0\) or \(\mathbf E=0\). For the total enclosed charge, use \(\oint\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf a=Q_{\mathrm{total,enc}}/\varepsilon_0\).

Derive both directions · D and free charge

Differential → Integral

\[\int_{\mathcal V}\nabla\cdot\mathbf D\,dV=\int_{\mathcal V}\rho_f\,dV \Rightarrow\boxed{\oint_{\partial\mathcal V}\mathbf D\cdot d\mathbf a=Q_{f,\mathrm{enc}}}.\]

Integral → Differential

\[\oint_{\partial\mathcal V}\mathbf D\cdot d\mathbf a=\int_{\mathcal V}\rho_f\,dV \Rightarrow\int_{\mathcal V}(\nabla\cdot\mathbf D-\rho_f)\,dV=0.\]

For arbitrary volumes, \(\boxed{\nabla\cdot\mathbf D=\rho_f}\). Here \(\rho_f\) excludes bound polarization charge.

II. Magnetic Gauss, in matter

Differential\[\boxed{\nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0}\]Integral\[\boxed{\oint_{\partial\mathcal V}\mathbf B\cdot d\mathbf a=0}\]

Unchanged from vacuum; the same two-way divergence-theorem derivation in Chapter 02 applies.

Physical meaning

Local meaning: magnetization changes the distribution of \(\mathbf B\), but does not create true magnetic monopoles. Thus \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf B\) remains zero in matter.

Global meaning: every closed surface has zero net \(\mathbf B\)-flux, even when the surface passes through a magnet or across a material interface. This law concerns \(\mathbf B\), not \(\mathbf H\); \(\mathbf H\) can have nonzero divergence in magnetized matter.

III. Faraday, in matter

Differential\[\boxed{\nabla\times\mathbf E=-\frac{\partial\mathbf B}{\partial t}}\]Integral, fixed loop\[\boxed{\oint_{\partial\mathcal S}\mathbf E\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell=-\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\mathcal S}\mathbf B\cdot d\mathbf a}\]

Unchanged from vacuum; the same two-way Stokes-theorem derivation in Chapter 02 applies.

Physical meaning

Local meaning: a time-dependent \(\mathbf B\) creates electric-field curl whether the region is vacuum, dielectric, or magnetic material. The induced electric field is \(\mathbf E\), not \(\mathbf D\).

Global meaning: a change of magnetic flux through a fixed loop is associated with circulation of \(\mathbf E\) around that loop. Materials can change the fields through polarization and magnetization, but they do not change the form of Faraday's law. The negative sign gives the Lenz-law orientation.

IV. Ampère–Maxwell, in matter

Differential\[\boxed{\nabla\times\mathbf H=\mathbf J_f+\frac{\partial\mathbf D}{\partial t}}\]Integral, fixed loop\[\boxed{\oint_{\partial\mathcal S}\mathbf H\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell=I_{f,\mathrm{enc}}+\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\mathcal S}\mathbf D\cdot d\mathbf a}\]

Physical meaning

Local meaning: the curl of \(\mathbf H\) is determined by free current density and the time derivative of \(\mathbf D\). The effects of magnetization currents are absorbed into \(\mathbf H\), while polarization current is included in \(\partial\mathbf D/\partial t\).

Global meaning: circulation of \(\mathbf H\) around a loop depends on free current passing through a spanning surface and changing \(\mathbf D\)-flux. Do not replace \(\mathbf H\) by \(\mathbf B/\mu_0\) or \(\mathbf D\) by \(\varepsilon_0\mathbf E\) inside matter without suitable constitutive assumptions.

Derive both directions · H, D, and free current

Differential → Integral

\[\int_{\mathcal S}(\nabla\times\mathbf H)\cdot d\mathbf a =\int_{\mathcal S}\mathbf J_f\cdot d\mathbf a +\int_{\mathcal S}\frac{\partial\mathbf D}{\partial t}\cdot d\mathbf a.\]

Use Stokes on the left; for a fixed surface bring the time derivative outside; and define \(I_{f,\mathrm{enc}}=\int_{\mathcal S}\mathbf J_f\cdot d\mathbf a\):

\[\boxed{\oint_{\partial\mathcal S}\mathbf H\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell=I_{f,\mathrm{enc}}+\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\mathcal S}\mathbf D\cdot d\mathbf a}.\]

Integral → Differential

Apply Stokes backward and express both terms on the right as surface integrals:

\[\int_{\mathcal S}\left(\nabla\times\mathbf H-\mathbf J_f-\frac{\partial\mathbf D}{\partial t}\right)\cdot d\mathbf a=0.\]

For every arbitrarily small oriented patch, the integrand vanishes:

\[\boxed{\nabla\times\mathbf H=\mathbf J_f+\frac{\partial\mathbf D}{\partial t}}.\]
Why the magnetic Gauss and Faraday equations do not change

Changing the description from microscopic sources to free and bound sources introduces \(\mathbf D\) and \(\mathbf H\) through \(\mathbf P,\mathbf M\). It does not introduce magnetic monopoles or change Faraday's induction law. Thus \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0\) and \(\nabla\times\mathbf E=-\partial_t\mathbf B\) keep the same form in ordinary macroscopic matter.

For these two equations the step-by-step forward and reverse conversions are exactly those already shown in the vacuum section; their validity is not restricted to empty space.

04 / MATERIAL RESPONSE

Where \(\mathbf D\) and \(\mathbf H\) come from

Material constants: \(\mathbf P\) (polarization) is \(\mathrm{C/m^2}\), \(\mathbf M\) (magnetization) is \(\mathrm{A/m}\), \(\varepsilon\) is \(\mathrm{F/m}\), and \(\mu\) is \(\mathrm{H/m}\).

Principle first: polarization and magnetization

Statement. Electric polarization \(\mathbf P\) is dipole moment per unit volume; magnetization \(\mathbf M\) is magnetic dipole moment per unit volume. Spatial variation and discontinuity of polarization are associated with bound charge, while magnetization produces bound currents.

Physical meaning. An electrically neutral dielectric can still change electric fields because its positive and negative charges shift slightly relative to each other. Similarly, microscopic circulating currents or magnetic moments can affect \(\mathbf B\) without a macroscopic free current through the sample.

When to apply simplified formulas. For homogeneous linear isotropic media, \(\mathbf P=\varepsilon_0\chi_e\mathbf E\) and a linear magnetic response may be described by an effective \(\mu\). Anisotropic, nonlinear, hysteretic, spatially varying or dispersive materials require more complete constitutive relations.

\[\boxed{\mathbf D=\varepsilon_0\mathbf E+\mathbf P}, \qquad\boxed{\mathbf H=\frac{\mathbf B}{\mu_0}-\mathbf M}.\]

\(\mathbf P\): electric dipole moment per volume (polarization). \(\mathbf M\): magnetic dipole moment per volume (magnetization).

Derive the macroscopic electric Gauss equation

Separate total charge: \(\rho=\rho_f+\rho_b\), with bound charge \(\rho_b=-\nabla\cdot\mathbf P\). Starting from microscopic Gauss's law:

\[\varepsilon_0\nabla\cdot\mathbf E=\rho_f+\rho_b=\rho_f-\nabla\cdot\mathbf P.\] \[\nabla\cdot(\varepsilon_0\mathbf E+\mathbf P)=\rho_f.\]

Define \(\mathbf D=\varepsilon_0\mathbf E+\mathbf P\):

\[\boxed{\nabla\cdot\mathbf D=\rho_f}.\]

The associated integral equation follows from the divergence theorem, as shown above.

Derive the macroscopic Ampère–Maxwell equation

Separate total current: \(\mathbf J=\mathbf J_f+\mathbf J_b\), with bound current

\[\mathbf J_b=\nabla\times\mathbf M+\frac{\partial\mathbf P}{\partial t}.\]

Insert into the microscopic/vacuum-form Ampère–Maxwell law:

\[\frac{1}{\mu_0}\nabla\times\mathbf B =\mathbf J_f+\nabla\times\mathbf M+\frac{\partial\mathbf P}{\partial t}+\varepsilon_0\frac{\partial\mathbf E}{\partial t}.\]

Collect a curl on the left and a time derivative on the right:

\[\nabla\times\left(\frac{\mathbf B}{\mu_0}-\mathbf M\right) =\mathbf J_f+\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(\varepsilon_0\mathbf E+\mathbf P).\]

By the definitions of \(\mathbf H\) and \(\mathbf D\):

\[\boxed{\nabla\times\mathbf H=\mathbf J_f+\frac{\partial\mathbf D}{\partial t}}.\]

Constitutive relations require assumptions

For a linear, isotropic, homogeneous medium with scalar constants \(\varepsilon,\mu\), one often uses

\[\mathbf D=\varepsilon\mathbf E,\qquad \mathbf B=\mu\mathbf H.\]

These relations are not universal. Anisotropic, dispersive, nonlinear, or inhomogeneous materials generally require more elaborate constitutive laws. Maxwell's equations themselves do not require \(\mathbf D\parallel\mathbf E\) or \(\mathbf B\parallel\mathbf H\).

05 / APPLICATIONS

Immediate results of Maxwell's equations

Exam practice: Study the eight EM comprehensive-exam questions and worked solutions covering Maxwell laws, dielectric and magnetic boundaries, magnetization, waves, reflection, and skin depth.

From Maxwell equations to useful predictions

Statement. Maxwell's equations imply field jump conditions at interfaces, waves that propagate even through charge-free vacuum, and conservation of electromagnetic energy. These results are consequences of the same four field equations rather than independent assumptions.

Physical interpretation. A field discontinuity reflects surface sources or material response; a wave reflects mutual time dependence of \(\mathbf E\) and \(\mathbf B\); the Poynting vector measures electromagnetic energy carried through space.

Conditions. Always distinguish static from time-harmonic problems, free-space relations from material constitutive laws, and lossless media from conductors. Wave speed \(1/\sqrt{\mu\varepsilon}\) and simple energy density formulas require the stated homogeneous, linear, lossless, nondispersive assumptions.

Common answers: electric field \(\mathrm{V/m}\), magnetic field \(\mathrm{T}\), phase speed \(\mathrm{m/s}\), field energy per volume \(\mathrm{J/m^3}\), and energy transferred per area per time \(\mathrm{W/m^2}\).

Further derivations and applications

Read how Maxwell's interface conditions lead to Snell's law, then review the wave equations for electric and magnetic fields, both Helmholtz equations, and electromagnetic energy density and Poynting's energy-transfer theorem.

Boundary conditions at an interface

Use a Gaussian pillbox for normal components and a thin rectangular loop for tangential components. The full electrostatic, magnetic and electromagnetic derivations are available on the dedicated Boundary Conditions page.

Derive the electromagnetic wave equation in vacuum

In a source-free region, \(\rho=0\), \(\mathbf J=0\). Curl Faraday's law:

\[\nabla\times(\nabla\times\mathbf E) =-\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(\nabla\times\mathbf B).\]

Use the vector identity and \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf E=0\):

\[\nabla\times(\nabla\times\mathbf E)=\nabla(\nabla\cdot\mathbf E)-\nabla^2\mathbf E=-\nabla^2\mathbf E.\]

Use \(\nabla\times\mathbf B=\mu_0\varepsilon_0\,\partial\mathbf E/\partial t\):

\[-\nabla^2\mathbf E=-\mu_0\varepsilon_0\frac{\partial^2\mathbf E}{\partial t^2}.\] \[\boxed{\nabla^2\mathbf E=\mu_0\varepsilon_0\frac{\partial^2\mathbf E}{\partial t^2}}.\]

Likewise \(\nabla^2\mathbf B=\mu_0\varepsilon_0\,\partial_t^2\mathbf B\). Thus the vacuum wave speed is \(c=1/\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0}\).

Example: a wave varying along \(z\) may have polarization \(\mathbf E=E_x(z,t)\hat{\mathbf x}\); the derivative direction and field-component direction need not coincide.

Electromagnetic energy · Poynting theorem

In vacuum, combining Maxwell's curl equations with \(\nabla\cdot(\mathbf E\times\mathbf B)=\mathbf B\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf E)-\mathbf E\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf B)\) gives

\[\boxed{\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot\mathbf S=-\mathbf J\cdot\mathbf E},\] \[u=\frac{\varepsilon_0E^2}{2}+\frac{B^2}{2\mu_0}, \qquad\mathbf S=\frac{1}{\mu_0}\mathbf E\times\mathbf B.\]

\(u\) is electromagnetic energy density, \(\mathbf S\) is energy flux, and \(\mathbf J\cdot\mathbf E\) is power transferred from fields to charges per volume.

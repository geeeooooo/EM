Electrostatics describes electric fields produced by time-independent charge distributions. Here \(\partial/\partial t=0\), \(\nabla\times\mathbf E=0\), and the electric field can be represented using a scalar potential \(V\). This page treats both vacuum and dielectrics.
01 / FIELD AND POTENTIAL

From Gauss's law to electric potential

Governing equations

\[\boxed{\nabla\cdot\mathbf E=\frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0}},\qquad \boxed{\nabla\times\mathbf E=\mathbf0}\] \[\boxed{\mathbf E=-\nabla V}\]

The divergence measures local electric sources and sinks. Zero curl means static electric fields are conservative, so their circulation around a closed path is zero.

Derive electric potential from zero curl

Begin with \(\nabla\times\mathbf E=0\). Apply Stokes' theorem to any fixed loop in a simply connected electrostatic region:

\[\oint_C\mathbf E\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell=\int_S(\nabla\times\mathbf E)\cdot d\mathbf a=0.\]

Consequently the line integral between points \(A\) and \(B\) is path-independent. Define

\[V(B)-V(A)=-\int_A^B\mathbf E\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell.\]

For an infinitesimal displacement \(d\mathbf r\), \(dV=-\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf r\). But by the total differential,

\[dV=\frac{\partial V}{\partial x}\,dx+\frac{\partial V}{\partial y}\,dy+\frac{\partial V}{\partial z}\,dz=\nabla V\cdot d\mathbf r.\]

Since this must hold for any displacement,

\[\boxed{\mathbf E=-\nabla V}.\]

Meaning: \(\mathbf E\) points toward decreasing potential; adding a constant to \(V\) changes no physical field (gauge freedom).

Gauss's law · a uniformly charged sphere

Suppose a sphere of radius \(R\) has uniform volume charge density \(\rho\). Symmetry makes \(\mathbf E=E(r)\hat{\mathbf r}\). Choose a spherical Gaussian surface of radius \(r\).

\[\oint\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf a=E(r)\,4\pi r^2=\frac{Q_{\mathrm{enc}}(r)}{\varepsilon_0}.\]

For \(r<R\), \(Q_{\mathrm{enc}}=\rho(4\pi r^3/3)\); for \(r\ge R\), \(Q_{\mathrm{enc}}=\rho(4\pi R^3/3)=Q\):

\[\boxed{E(r)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{\rho r}{3\varepsilon_0},&r<R,\\[4pt] \dfrac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2},&r\ge R.\end{cases}}\]

This method uses symmetry; Gauss's law always holds, but not every geometry allows it to determine \(E\) so easily.

02 / BOUNDARY-VALUE EQUATIONS

Derive Poisson's and Laplace's equations

Vacuum · from Gauss's law to Poisson

Start with Gauss's law and the electrostatic potential:

\[\nabla\cdot\mathbf E=\frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0},\qquad\mathbf E=-\nabla V.\]

Substitute and use \(\nabla\cdot\nabla V=\nabla^2V\):

\[-\nabla\cdot(\nabla V)=\frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0}\] \[\boxed{\nabla^2V=-\frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0}}\quad\text{(Poisson)}.\]

In a region with no volume charge, \(\rho=0\):

\[\boxed{\nabla^2V=0}\quad\text{(Laplace)}.\]

Important: a charge-free region can still have a nonzero \(\mathbf E\) and nonconstant \(V\), because boundary charges or sources outside that region can produce fields inside.

Material version · why variable permittivity matters

In an isotropic linear dielectric, \(\mathbf D=\varepsilon(\mathbf r)\mathbf E\), and \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf D=\rho_f\). With \(\mathbf E=-\nabla V\),

\[-\nabla\cdot[\varepsilon(\mathbf r)\nabla V]=\rho_f.\] \[\boxed{\nabla\cdot[\varepsilon(\mathbf r)\nabla V]=-\rho_f}.\]

Expand with the product rule:

\[\varepsilon\nabla^2V+(\nabla\varepsilon)\cdot(\nabla V)=-\rho_f.\]

Only when \(\varepsilon\) is spatially constant in the region may we write \(\nabla^2V=-\rho_f/\varepsilon\). Interfaces additionally require the boundary conditions on \(V\) and \(\mathbf D\).

03 / SOLVED LAPLACE PROBLEM

Two parallel plates

Two infinite conducting plates are at \(x=0\) and \(x=a\), with \(V(0)=0\), \(V(a)=V_0\). The dielectric between them is homogeneous and contains no free volume charge. Edge effects are neglected.

Step-by-step solution and surface charge

By translational symmetry the potential depends only on \(x\), and Laplace's equation becomes

\[\frac{d^2V}{dx^2}=0.\]

Integrate once: \(dV/dx=C_1\). Integrate again: \(V(x)=C_1x+C_2\).

Apply \(V(0)=0\Rightarrow C_2=0\) and \(V(a)=V_0\Rightarrow C_1=V_0/a\):

\[\boxed{V(x)=\frac{V_0x}{a}}.\]

Differentiate to obtain the electric field:

\[\boxed{\mathbf E=-\nabla V=-\frac{V_0}{a}\hat{\mathbf x}}.\]

For a linear dielectric \(\mathbf D=\varepsilon\mathbf E\), the free charge density on a conductor face is \(\sigma_f=\mathbf D_{\mathrm{outside}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}_{\mathrm{out}}\). At \(x=0\) the conductor normal into the gap is \(+\hat{\mathbf x}\); at \(x=a\) it is \(-\hat{\mathbf x}\):

\[\boxed{\sigma_{x=0}=-\frac{\varepsilon V_0}{a}},\qquad \boxed{\sigma_{x=a}=+\frac{\varepsilon V_0}{a}}.\]

Check: when the right plate is at higher potential, the electric field points leftward, from the positive plate to the negative plate.

04 / SOLVED POISSON PROBLEM

Uniform volume charge between plates

Keep \(V(0)=0\), \(V(a)=V_0\), but now add a uniform free volume charge density \(\rho_f\) in a homogeneous dielectric with permittivity \(\varepsilon\).

Solve the potential and electric field explicitly
\[\frac{d^2V}{dx^2}=-\frac{\rho_f}{\varepsilon}.\]

Integrate twice:

\[\frac{dV}{dx}=-\frac{\rho_fx}{\varepsilon}+C_1,\] \[V(x)=-\frac{\rho_fx^2}{2\varepsilon}+C_1x+C_2.\]

The first boundary condition gives \(C_2=0\). The second gives

\[V_0=-\frac{\rho_fa^2}{2\varepsilon}+C_1a\quad\Rightarrow\quad C_1=\frac{V_0}{a}+\frac{\rho_fa}{2\varepsilon}.\]

Therefore

\[\boxed{V(x)=\frac{V_0x}{a}+\frac{\rho_f}{2\varepsilon}(ax-x^2)}.\] \[\boxed{\mathbf E(x)=\left[-\frac{V_0}{a}+\frac{\rho_f}{\varepsilon}\left(x-\frac{a}{2}\right)\right]\hat{\mathbf x}}.\]

Check: taking \(\rho_f\to0\) recovers the Laplace problem.

05 / BOUNDARY CONDITIONS AND UNIQUENESS

How boundary data determine the solution

Electrostatic interface

\[\boxed{V_2=V_1},\qquad \boxed{\varepsilon_2\partial_nV_2-\varepsilon_1\partial_nV_1=-\sigma_f}\]

Assumes an ordinary interface (no ideal dipole layer) and linear, isotropic dielectrics. Tangential \(\mathbf E\) is continuous; free surface charge controls the jump in normal \(\mathbf D\). See the full pillbox/loop derivation.

Why the Dirichlet solution is unique

Let \(V_1,V_2\) be two solutions of Laplace's equation on the same connected bounded region, with the same specified potential on the entire boundary. Their difference \(W=V_1-V_2\) satisfies

\[\nabla^2W=0,\qquad W|_{\partial\mathcal V}=0.\]

Use the identity

\[\nabla\cdot(W\nabla W)=|\nabla W|^2+W\nabla^2W.\]

Integrate over the region and apply the divergence theorem:

\[\oint_{\partial\mathcal V}W\nabla W\cdot d\mathbf a =\int_{\mathcal V}|\nabla W|^2\,dV+\int_{\mathcal V}W\nabla^2W\,dV.\]

The surface term is zero because \(W=0\) at the boundary, and the last volume term is zero by Laplace's equation:

\[0=\int_{\mathcal V}|\nabla W|^2\,dV.\]

The nonnegative integrand must vanish, so \(\nabla W=0\). Thus \(W\) is constant, and because it vanishes on the boundary, \(W=0\) everywhere.

Meaning: matching both the differential equation and complete boundary data determines the electrostatic potential uniquely.
